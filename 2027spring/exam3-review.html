Chapter 5 — Merchandising Operations

📖 For worked examples and full detail, see the Study Guide — Chapter 5.

Record purchases AND sales using the GROSS method — this is what ALL assignments and exams use (per the Chapter 5 Additional Study Note). The video lectures showed sales under the Net method, but that is appendix-only (Appendix 5B / LO 5.8) and is NOT tested. Sales below are shown gross, with Sales Discount as a contra-revenue.

Key Ideas

  • Merchandiser vs. service company. A merchandiser buys finished goods and resells them at a markup (buy $70, sell $100 → $30 gross profit). It reports Merchandise Inventory, a permanent asset account a service company doesn't have.
  • Every sale = TWO entries: (1) revenue at the selling price, (2) COGS at the cost (Cr. Merchandise Inventory). The single most-repeated point — never forget the COGS entry.
  • Inventory acts like a prepaid expense: paid up front, held as an asset, expensed as COGS only when sold.
  • Perpetual system only (continuous running record; COGS at every sale). A physical count is still done to catch shrinkage (count < books). Periodic is appendix/not tested.
  • Credit terms (e.g., 2/10, n/30): 2% off if paid within 10 days, else full amount due in 30. Offered by the seller to speed up collection.
  • FOB terms decide who owns goods in transit on 12/31 and who pays freight. Shipping Point → buyer owns in transit, buyer pays = Freight-in. Destination → seller owns in transit, seller pays = Freight-out.
  • Freight-in is capitalized into inventory (expensed later via COGS). Freight-out is an operating Delivery Expense for the seller.
  • Return vs. allowance: a return brings goods back (inventory + COGS effect); an allowance is a price cut with no goods returned (revenue side only). Sales R&A also involve a year-end estimate, then later the actual entries — actuals have no income-statement effect (already estimated).
  • Multi-step income statement: Net Sales − COGS = Gross Profit − Operating Expenses = Operating Income ± Other = Pre-tax − Tax = Net Income.

Key Formulas & Rules

  • Net Purchases = Purchases − Purchase Returns & Allowances − Purchase Discounts (freight-in is NOT in this figure, but IS added to inventory cost)
  • Cost of Goods Available for Sale = Beginning Inventory + Net Purchases + Freight-in
  • Beginning Inv + Net Purchases + Freight-in = COGS + Ending Inv (memorize)
  • Net Sales = Sales Revenue − Sales Returns & Allowances − Sales Discounts
  • Gross Profit = Net Sales − COGS
  • Gross Profit % = Gross Profit ÷ Net Sales
  • Discount is computed on the AP balance AFTER returns, never the original invoice
  • Freight is never discountable; revenue uses the selling price, COGS uses the cost (always two different numbers)

Must-Know Journal Entries

(Perpetual; purchases and sales both GROSS method.)

  • Purchase on account: Dr. Merchandise Inventory / Cr. Accounts Payable
  • Pay WITHIN discount period: Dr. Accounts Payable 100 / Cr. Cash 98 / Cr. Merchandise Inventory 2 (discount reduces the asset's cost; pay after window → Dr. A/P / Cr. Cash at full)
  • Sale on account (two entries): Dr. Accounts Receivable 100 / Cr. Sales Revenue 100 and Dr. Cost of Goods Sold 70 / Cr. Merchandise Inventory 70
  • Collect WITHIN discount period: Dr. Cash 98 / Dr. Sales Discount 2 / Cr. Accounts Receivable 100 (after window → Dr. Cash 100 / Cr. A/R 100)

Common Mistakes

  • Forgetting the second (COGS) entry on a sale — every sale is two entries.
  • Mixing up sales price vs. cost — revenue at selling price, COGS at original cost; never the same number.
  • Applying the discount to the wrong base — compute it on the AP balance after any return.
  • Discounting freight — freight is never eligible for the purchase discount.
  • Confusing Freight-in with Freight-out — Freight-in → inventory asset (buyer, FOB Shipping Point); Freight-out → Delivery Expense (seller, FOB Destination).
  • Wrong owner of goods in transit on 12/31 — Shipping Point = buyer's books; Destination = seller's books.
  • Recording revenue/COGS again on an actual return — double-counts; actuals only clear Refunds Payable and Estimated Returns Inventory.
  • Adding a cost-side entry to an allowance — no goods back → revenue/refund side only.
  • Crediting a purchase discount to income — it's credited to Merchandise Inventory (reduces cost), not revenue.
  • Treating "Purchases" as an inventory account — under perpetual, debit Merchandise Inventory directly.
  • Using Sales Discounts Forfeited (Net method) on assignments/exams — that's appendix-only; use the gross Sales Discount contra-revenue instead.
  • Leaving Freight-in out of the COGS equation — it's added to Goods Available for Sale even though it isn't part of "Net Purchases."

Exam Checklist

  • ☐ You should be able to record a sale with both entries (revenue at price; COGS at cost).
  • ☐ You should be able to record purchases using the GROSS method, handling pay-within vs. pay-after (discount → Cr. Merchandise Inventory).
  • ☐ You should be able to record sales using the GROSS method (A/R & Sales at full price; if paid within the window → Dr. Sales Discount) — the method used on all assignments and exams.
  • ☐ You should be able to compute a purchase discount on the post-return AP balance and exclude freight.
  • ☐ You should be able to classify freight: Freight-in → Merchandise Inventory; Freight-out → Delivery Expense.
  • ☐ You should be able to determine ownership of goods in transit by FOB term (Shipping Point = buyer; Destination = seller).
  • ☐ You should be able to handle Sales Returns & Allowances: the year-end estimate (Refunds Payable / Estimated Returns Inventory) and the actual entries, knowing actuals have no I/S effect.
  • ☐ You should be able to distinguish a return (goods back → inventory + COGS) from an allowance (no goods → revenue side only).
  • ☐ You should be able to record inventory shrinkage: Dr. COGS / Cr. Merchandise Inventory.
  • ☐ You should be able to build a multi-step income statement and apply Beg. Inv + Net Purchases + Freight-in = COGS + End. Inv.
  • ☐ You should be able to compute Gross Profit % = Gross Profit ÷ Net Sales.
  • ☐ You should be able to use the Perpetual system throughout (periodic is appendix/not tested).

Chapter 6 — Merchandise Inventory

📖 For worked examples and full detail, see the Study Guide — Chapter 6.

Scope: Periodic system (LO 6.7 appendix) is NOT tested — know the concept only.

Key Ideas

  • The costing problem. Recording purchases/sales is easy; the hard part is assigning Cost of Goods Sold (COGS) when identical units are bought at different prices over time. As long as unit cost never changes, COGS is trivial — but prices rise (inflation), so costs change.
  • Specific Identification is used for unique, high-value items (cars, jewelry, real estate) where you know the actual unit sold. It is NOT a cost flow assumption — no assumption is needed.
  • Cost flow assumptions apply to many identical mixed units: FIFO, LIFO, Weighted-Average. They describe the flow of costs, not the physical flow of goods.
  • FIFO — oldest costs → COGS; newest costs stay in Ending Inventory.
  • LIFO — newest costs → COGS; oldest costs stay in Ending Inventory.
  • Weighted-Average — blend all costs into one average unit cost. Perpetual version = "moving average": recompute after every purchase only, never after a sale.
  • Rising-cost effects: FIFO gives highest profit & ending inventory; LIFO gives lowest (and lowest tax). Avg is in the middle. (If costs fall, the pattern flips.)
  • LCM / LCNRV — report inventory at the lower of cost vs. market; write down only, never write up (conservatism).
  • Inventory errors self-correct. Period 1 ending inventory = Period 2 beginning inventory, so an error reverses next period; total over two years is correct, but each year is individually misstated.
  • Four inventory principles: Consistency (same method each period), Disclosure (state method in notes), Materiality (don't track trivial items as inventory), Conservatism (basis for LCM/LCNRV).

Key Formulas & Rules

  • Goods Available for Sale = Beginning Inventory + Net Purchases
  • COGS = Goods Available − Ending Inventory
  • Ending Inventory = Goods Available − COGS (use to verify any method)
  • Average Unit Cost = Total Cost of Goods Available ÷ Total Units Available
  • Inventory Turnover = COGS ÷ Average Inventory, where Average Inventory = (Beginning + Ending) ÷ 2. Higher = sells faster.
  • Days' Sales in Inventory = 365 ÷ Inventory Turnover. Lower = sells faster.
  • LCM/LCNRV: FIFO/weighted-avg use lower of cost and NRV (selling price − costs to complete & sell); LIFO/retail use traditional LCM. Write down only if market < cost.

Rising-cost effects:

Method COGS Gross Profit / Net Income Ending Inventory Income Tax
FIFO Lowest Highest Highest Highest
Weighted-Avg Middle Middle Middle Middle
LIFO Highest Lowest Lowest Lowest

Must-Know Journal Entries

Purchase: Dr. Merchandise Inventory / Cr. Cash or Accounts Payable.

Sale (always TWO entries):

  • Dr. Accounts Receivable / Cr. Sales Revenue (selling price)
  • Dr. Cost of Goods Sold / Cr. Merchandise Inventory (cost — chosen method sets this amount)

The format is identical under FIFO, LIFO, and Weighted-Avg; only the COGS/Inventory dollar amount changes.

LCM / LCNRV write-down: Dr. Cost of Goods Sold / Cr. Merchandise Inventory. Recognize the decline as expense immediately, even with no sale. If market rises above cost → no entry.

Common Mistakes

  • Confusing physical flow with cost flow. LIFO doesn't mean you ship the newest goods; it's a cost assumption only.
  • Calling Specific Identification a cost flow assumption. It isn't — actual costs are known.
  • Recording only one entry on a sale. A sale needs TWO: the revenue entry AND the COGS/inventory entry.
  • Mixing up which method gives highest profit. When costs rise: FIFO = highest profit + highest ending inventory; LIFO = lowest of both. (Costs falling flips it.)
  • Writing inventory UP under LCM — only write down, never up (would anticipate a gain).
  • Forgetting moving-average recalculates only after a PURCHASE, not after a sale.
  • Forgetting the Period-1-ending = Period-2-beginning link when analyzing inventory errors.
  • Using the wrong "average" in turnover — it's (Beg + End) ÷ 2, not just ending inventory.
  • Periodic vs. perpetual timing — periodic records NO COGS at sale; COGS computed once at period-end after a physical count.

Exam Checklist

  • ☐ You should be able to record the purchase and the two-part sale journal entries (Sales = price, COGS = cost).
  • ☐ You should be able to compute COGS and Ending Inventory under FIFO, LIFO, and Weighted-Average (perpetual), including the moving-average schedule.
  • ☐ You should be able to recompute moving average after each purchase only.
  • ☐ You should be able to verify results with Goods Available = COGS + Ending Inventory.
  • ☐ You should be able to state the rising-cost effects table from memory (FIFO highest profit/EI; LIFO lowest; tax opposite).
  • ☐ You should be able to explain why FIFO/LIFO produce their results (cheap old costs vs. expensive new costs).
  • ☐ You should be able to apply LCM/LCNRV: pick lower of cost vs. market; write-down JE = Dr. COGS / Cr. Merchandise Inventory; no write-ups.
  • ☐ You should be able to trace an inventory error through COGS → Gross Profit → Net Income for both periods, and know it self-corrects after two years.
  • ☐ You should be able to compute Inventory Turnover and Days' Sales in Inventory and interpret high vs. low.
  • ☐ You should be able to name the four principles (Consistency, Disclosure, Materiality, Conservatism).
  • ☐ You should know the perpetual vs. periodic concept (periodic appendix NOT tested).

Chapter 7 — Internal Control and Cash

📖 For worked examples and full detail, see the Study Guide — Chapter 7.

Exam-prep summary. The professor is selective: he skipped petty cash and credit card sales (L.O. 4 & 5) and will NOT test or assign homework on them. The lecture's focus is L.O. 6: Bank Reconciliation — study that hard.

Key Ideas

  • Internal control (4-part purpose): a plan and measures to (1) safeguard assets, (2) encourage compliance with company policies, (3) promote operational efficiency, and (4) ensure accurate, reliable accounting records. Know all four.
  • Bank as a control device: depositing cash creates a double record (your books + the bank), giving an independent check. Comparing them is the bank reconciliation.
  • Why balances differ: the book Cash balance and bank statement rarely match, due to (1) timing differences (e.g., a deposit in transit recorded by you but not yet by the bank) and (2) errors (yours or the bank's).
  • Two-sided logic — memorize: Bank side = items the company already recorded but the bank hasn't processed (deposits in transit, outstanding checks) → books are already right → NO journal entry. Book side = items the bank already processed but the company hasn't recorded (service charges, NSF, collections, EFT, interest) → journal entries REQUIRED.
  • Quick test: "Who already knows about this?" Company only → Bank side. Bank only → Book side. Errors go on the side that made the mistake.
  • Debit/Credit memos (from the BANK's view — your account is its liability): Credit memo = bank increased your account (collections, interest, EFT receipts) → ADD on Book side. Debit memo = bank decreased it (service charges, NSF) → SUBTRACT on Book side. This is opposite of your own debit/credit intuition.
  • Report the adjusted (true) cash balance on the balance sheet — not the raw book or bank figure.

Key Formulas & Rules

Both sides must reach the same adjusted ("true") cash balance.

BANK SIDE (no journal entries) BOOK SIDE (journal entries required)
Start: balance per bank statement Start: balance per books (Cash ledger)
+ Deposits in transit + Collections (notes + interest), EFT receipts, interest revenue
− Outstanding checks − Service charges, NSF checks, EFT payments
+/− Bank errors +/− Book errors
= Adjusted bank balance = Adjusted book balance
  • Only the Book side requires journal entries. Writing "+1,035" on the reconciliation sheet does NOT update the ledger — journalize each Book-side item.
  • NSF rule: a bounced check reduces cash AND reinstates an Account Receivable (the customer still owes you).
  • Book error direction: decide whether the error overstated or understated cash. Recorded a payment too high → you removed too much cash → ADD it back.

Must-Know Journal Entries

(Book-side items only — Bank-side items get no entry.)

  • Collection of a note (net of fee): Dr. Cash, Dr. Misc. Expense (collection fee) / Cr. Notes Receivable, Cr. Interest Revenue.
  • NSF check: Dr. Accounts Receivable / Cr. Cash.
  • Bank service charge: Dr. Miscellaneous Expense / Cr. Cash.
  • Book error correction: Dr. or Cr. Cash for the difference, with the offsetting account (e.g., Accounts Payable).

Common Mistakes

  • Journalizing Bank-side items. Deposits in transit and outstanding checks were already recorded — re-recording double-counts. No entry.
  • Forgetting that ONLY the Book side needs entries — the #1 tested distinction.
  • Putting an item on the wrong side. Use "Who already knows?" If unsure on a memo: credit memo → add on Book; debit memo → subtract on Book.
  • Reversing debit/credit memo direction. They're from the bank's view: credit memo = MORE cash for you; debit memo = LESS.
  • NSF → just reducing cash. You must also debit Accounts Receivable.
  • Wrong book-error direction. Recorded payment too high → ADD cash back; too low → subtract.
  • Putting a bank error on the Book side (or vice versa). Error goes on the side of whoever made it.
  • Reporting the wrong cash figure — report the adjusted balance.

Exam Checklist

  • ☐ You should be able to state the 4-part internal control definition (safeguard assets, encourage policy compliance, promote efficiency, ensure reliable records).
  • ☐ You should be able to explain why book and bank balances differ: timing differences + errors.
  • ☐ You should be able to build a full reconciliation with the correct starting points and items, both sides tying to the same true cash balance.
  • ☐ You should be able to place Bank-side items correctly: + deposits in transit, − outstanding checks, +/− bank errors → NO journal entries.
  • ☐ You should be able to place Book-side items correctly: + collections/EFT receipts/interest, − service charges/NSF/EFT payments, +/− book errors → journal entries REQUIRED.
  • ☐ You should be able to identify which reconciling items trigger an adjusting entry (Book-side items only).
  • ☐ You should be able to journalize the four classic Book-side entries from memory (note collection, book error, NSF, service charge).
  • ☐ You should be able to apply credit memo = add / debit memo = subtract (Book side, from the bank's view).
  • ☐ You should be able to handle an NSF check: Dr. Accounts Receivable / Cr. Cash.
  • ☐ You should be able to confirm the adjusted Cash balance equals the adjusted bank balance.
  • ☐ (Skipped — not tested: petty cash, credit card sales journalizing.)

Chapter 8 — Receivables

📖 For worked examples and full detail, see the Study Guide — Chapter 8.

Scope note: detailed Notes Receivable exact-day interest computations are largely skipped. Required takeaway only: a note bears interest, and interest accrues over time.

Key Ideas

  • A/R vs N/R: A/R ("trade receivables") are due in 30–60 days, no formal document, usually no interest. N/R ("promissory notes") are longer-term, evidenced by a legally binding written document, and bear interest. Only the note bears interest and has a formal document.
  • Why estimate uncollectibles? Selling to many customers means some won't pay. Reporting full A/R overstates assets — violates conservatism. Estimating losses keeps A/R realistic.
  • Allowance Method (GAAP) vs Direct Write-off: Direct write-off records the loss only when an account is deemed uncollectible — landing the expense in a later period than the sale, which violates the matching principle. The Allowance Method records estimated Bad Debt Expense via an end-of-period adjusting entry, matching it to the same period's sales.
  • Allowance for Bad Debts (= Doubtful Accounts) is a contra-asset with a normal CREDIT balance. It's used because at estimate time you know the aggregate dollars likely uncollectible but not which specific customer will default.
  • NRV = A/R − Allowance — the cash you realistically expect to collect.
  • Three estimation methods: Percent-of-sales is an income-statement approach — it records Bad Debt Expense directly and ignores the existing allowance balance. Percent-of-receivables and aging are balance-sheet approaches — they compute a target allowance balance, then plug the expense to reach it.
  • Reducing collection risk: factoring (sell A/R outright, transfers risk), pledging (borrow using A/R as collateral). Internal control: separate cash-handling from cash-accounting duties.

Key Formulas & Rules

Method Approach Computes directly Base Unadjusted balance
Percent-of-Sales Income statement Bad Debt Expense Net credit sales Ignored
Percent-of-Receivables Balance sheet Target Allowance Ending A/R × % Used (plug)
Aging-of-Receivables Balance sheet Target Allowance A/R by age bucket Used (plug)
  • Percent-of-sales: Bad Debt Expense = Net Credit Sales × % (record directly, ignore existing allowance).
  • Receivables/Aging: Target Allowance = Ending A/R × % (aging: sum each bucket × its own %, higher % for older). Bad Debt Expense = plug needed to move the unadjusted allowance to the target.
  • Unadjusted balance = beginning balance − write-offs (can be credit OR debit). From a debit unadjusted, the plug = target + debit amount (you climb from negative up to the positive target).
  • NRV = A/R − Allowance.
  • Interest = Principal × Rate × Time; Maturity Value = Principal + Interest.
  • Acid-Test = (Cash + S-T Investments + Net Receivables) ÷ Current Liabilities (1.0+ safe).
  • A/R Turnover = Net Credit Sales ÷ Average Net A/R; Days' Sales in Receivables = 365 ÷ Turnover (shorter = better).

Must-Know Journal Entries

  • Allowance adjusting entry: Dr. Bad Debt Expense / Cr. Allowance for Bad Debts.
  • Write-off (allowance method): Dr. Allowance for Bad Debts / Cr. Accounts Receivable—Customer. A write-off never touches Bad Debt Expense and leaves NRV unchanged (A/R and Allowance fall by the same amount).
  • Recovery (two steps): (1) reinstate — Dr. A/R / Cr. Allowance; (2) collect — Dr. Cash / Cr. A/R.
  • A/R → N/R conversion: Dr. Notes Receivable / Cr. Accounts Receivable.

Common Mistakes

  • Debiting Bad Debt Expense at write-off under the allowance method. Wrong — write-off is Dr. Allowance / Cr. A/R, with no income-statement effect; the expense was recorded in the prior adjusting entry.
  • Thinking a write-off changes NRV. It does not — A/R and Allowance drop equally.
  • Treating percent-of-receivables/aging like percent-of-sales — recording the target balance as the expense instead of plugging.
  • Ignoring the unadjusted allowance balance in the plug methods, or using it in percent-of-sales (where it must be ignored).
  • Mishandling a DEBIT unadjusted balance — from a $55 debit to a $185 credit target the expense is $240, not $130; you must cover the negative AND reach the target.
  • Using total sales instead of NET CREDIT SALES in percent-of-sales and A/R turnover.
  • Confusing A/R and N/R — only the note bears interest and has a formal document.
  • Forgetting the customer name on credit-sale and write-off entries.
  • Skipping the two-step recovery — reinstate first, then collect; don't jump straight to Dr. Cash.

Exam Checklist

  • ☐ You should be able to state the differences between A/R and N/R (term, document, interest).
  • ☐ You should be able to explain why direct write-off violates matching and why allowance is GAAP.
  • ☐ You should be able to record the allowance adjusting entry (Dr. Bad Debt Expense / Cr. Allowance).
  • ☐ You should be able to record an allowance write-off (Dr. Allowance / Cr. A/R) and explain no I/S effect + NRV unchanged.
  • ☐ You should be able to record a two-step recovery and identify which method hits the income statement (direct) vs not (allowance).
  • ☐ You should be able to compute Bad Debt Expense by percent-of-sales (Net Credit Sales × %, ignore allowance).
  • ☐ You should be able to compute the target balance and plug for percent-of-receivables and aging — and draw the T-account to find the plug, practicing both credit and debit unadjusted balances.
  • ☐ You should be able to build/read an aging schedule (sum buckets × their %s → target).
  • ☐ You should be able to state that Allowance is a contra-asset (normal credit balance) and NRV = A/R − Allowance.
  • ☐ You should be able to handle the write-off > allowance case (same entry → temporary debit balance, fixed at the next adjusting entry).
  • ☐ You should be able to identify the income-statement vs balance-sheet approach for each estimation method.
  • ☐ You should be able to compute and interpret the three ratios (acid-test, A/R turnover, days' sales).
  • ☐ You should be able to distinguish factoring vs pledging and explain the segregation-of-duties control.
  • ☐ You should be able to apply the N/R takeaway: Interest = Principal × Rate × Time, Maturity Value = Principal + Interest (detailed day-count math is skipped).
