Chapter 1 — Accounting & the Business Environment

Key Concepts

Accounting = "the language of business." It is an information system that measures business activities, processes them into reports, and communicates results to decision-makers. Because it's the language of business, every business student (marketing, strategy, finance) must learn it.

Two branches:

  • Financial accounting (this course, ACCT 2101) — serves external users. The professor stresses the two most important external users are creditors and investors (taxing authorities also use it).
  • Managerial accounting (ACCT 2102) — serves internal users (managers, employees) for budgets/strategy.

Objective of financial accounting: provide useful information to external users to aid their decision-making. Creditors and investors both finance the company, but for different reasons:

User Why they provide capital What they want
Creditors Lend money Earn interest + collect principal at maturity → need assurance the firm can repay
Investors Buy ownership (stock) Stock price growth (sell at profit) and/or dividends; want the company to grow and avoid bankruptcy. No guaranteed interest.

The course's "ultimate goal" (professor emphasizes repeatedly): to analyze financial statements. But you can't analyze what you don't understand, so you first learn to prepare them. Chapters 1–4 are the foundational, sequential chapters ("like mathematics") — master each before the next.

What makes information "useful" — Qualitative Characteristics:

Two primary characteristics:

  1. Relevance — must be capable of making a difference in a decision. Two ingredients: - Predictive value (helps predict future outcomes) - Confirmatory value (confirm/correct past predictions) - Related: Materiality — info is material if omitting/misstating it could influence a user's decision. Professor's example: a $10 error in a billion-dollar company is not material.
  2. Faithful Representation — must be: - Complete (nothing important omitted) - Neutral (not biased/manipulated to favor one group) - Free from error (reasonably accurate)

Four enhancing characteristics:

  • Comparability — compare Company A vs. Company B (this is why GAAP exists). Related: Consistency = same company applies same rules year over year.
  • Verifiability — independent auditors examining the same facts reach the same results.
  • Timeliness — delivered fast enough to be useful (10-year-old info is useless).
  • Understandability — clear enough for anyone with reasonable business knowledge ("you shouldn't need a PhD in accounting").

Governing bodies:

  • SEC (Securities and Exchange Commission) — government agency overseeing U.S. financial markets; has legal authority to set standards but delegated rule-making.
  • FASB (Financial Accounting Standards Board) — private body that actually creates/updates GAAP.
  • GAAP = Generally Accepted Accounting Principles (U.S. rules; focus of this course).
  • IASB (International Accounting Standards Board) → publishes IFRS (used outside the U.S.).
  • Flow: SEC → delegates → FASB → creates → GAAP.

Sarbanes-Oxley Act (SOX), 2002 — passed after the Enron scandal (one of the biggest accounting frauds in history). Key points the professor highlights:

  • Created the PCAOB (Public Company Accounting Oversight Board) — independent body that oversees/audits the auditors of public companies (auditors must register with it).
  • Increased penalties for white-collar crime; strengthened auditor independence.
  • Background: Arthur Andersen provided both auditing and consulting to Enron → major conflict of interest. SOX restricts firms from providing both services to the same client.

Four basic assumptions in financial reporting:

Assumption Meaning Professor's example
Monetary Unit Only items measurable in money are recorded A great CEO's leadership skills can't be put on the balance sheet
Economic Entity Business is separate from its owners/other businesses Owner's personal transactions stay out of company records
Periodicity Economic life is divided into artificial periods Annual & quarterly reporting
Going Concern Business will continue operating for the foreseeable future Statements assume no imminent bankruptcy

Forms of business organization (memorize the liability/tax differences — heavily emphasized):

Form # Owners Liability Taxation Life
Sole Proprietorship 1 (proprietor) Unlimited (personally liable) Pass-through (owner pays) Ends at owner's choice/death
Partnership 2+ Unlimited (partners personally liable) Pass-through to partners Ends at partner's choice/death
Corporation Up to thousands (stockholders) Limited (only lose your investment) Double taxation (corp tax + dividend tax) Indefinite
LLC 1+ members Limited Pass-through (no double tax) (Hybrid; varies by state)
  • Corporation = separate legal entity organized under state law. Key advantage: can raise huge capital (issue bonds or stock to the public). Limited liability example: invest $100, firm goes bankrupt → you only lose $100, even if the firm has massive debt.
  • Double taxation: corporation pays tax on income; stockholders pay personal tax again on dividends.
  • LLC = hybrid: taxed like a partnership (pass-through, no double tax) but with the limited liability of a corporation. Remember: "partnership for tax, corporation for liability."

Corporate ownership structure: buying stock makes you an owner with voting rights → stockholders elect the Board of Directors → the Board appoints CEO/CFO and top management who run the company. (Ownership ≠ direct management.)

Three types of business activities (seen on the balance sheet):

  • Operating — day-to-day: current assets (inventory, A/R) and current liabilities (wages payable).
  • Investing — buying/selling long-term assets: land, buildings, equipment (non-current assets).
  • Financing — raising capital: issuing long-term bonds (non-current liabilities) or issuing stock (equity).

Key Formulas & Rules

The Accounting Equation (the basic tool of accounting — must always be in balance, left = right):

$$\text{Assets} = \text{Liabilities} + \text{Stockholders' Equity}$$

  • Left side (Assets) = how the money is used (the resources).
  • Right side = where the money came from (claims): Liabilities = money from creditors; Stockholders' Equity = money from investors.

Account-identification rules (professor's shortcuts):

  • "Receivable" in the name → it's an Asset (right to receive money). Ex: Accounts Receivable, Notes Receivable.
  • "Payable" in the name → it's a Liability (obligation to pay). Ex: Accounts Payable, Notes Payable, Salaries Payable.
  • "On account" → creates Accounts Payable (if you buy) or Accounts Receivable (if you sell/perform).

Stockholders' Equity = two components:

  • Contributed Capital = funds invested directly by owners when stock is issued (account: Common Stock).
  • Earned Capital = Retained Earnings = accumulated profits the company has kept over its entire life.

Retained Earnings is affected by: Revenues (↑), Expenses (↓), Dividends (↓).

The Four Financial Statements & how they connect (prepared in this order):

  1. Income Statement — Revenues − Expenses = Net Income (or Net Loss). Reports results FOR a period of time. Preparation always starts here.
  2. Statement of Retained Earnings — the bridge from Income Statement to Balance Sheet. Reports FOR a period.

$$\text{Beginning R/E} + \text{Net Income} - \text{Dividends} = \text{Ending R/E}$$

  1. Balance Sheet — Assets = Liabilities + Equity. Reports financial position AS OF a specific date (a point in time). The Ending R/E flows into the Equity section here.
  2. Statement of Cash Flows — cash inflows/outflows in three categories (operating, investing, financing); ties beginning cash to ending cash. Reports FOR a period. (Detailed prep covered later.)

Connection flow: Net Income (Income Stmt) → Statement of R/E → Ending R/E → Equity on Balance Sheet.

Worked example — R/E flowing across years (professor's example):

  • Year 1 (first year of operations): Beginning R/E = $0; Net Income = $100; Dividends = $0 → Ending R/E = $100 → flows to Year 1 Balance Sheet equity.
  • Year 2: Beginning R/E = $100 (= Year 1 ending); Net Income = $150; Dividends = $0 → Ending R/E = $250.
  • Takeaway: R/E = cumulative net income retained, minus dividends, over the company's entire life.

Timeframe rule (commonly tested): Income Statement, Statement of R/E, and Statement of Cash Flows are "for the period ended..."; only the Balance Sheet is "as of [date]".

Return on Assets (ROA) — the chapter's ratio:

$$\text{ROA} = \frac{\text{Net Income}}{\text{Average Total Assets}}$$

  • Use average total assets (beginning + ending) ÷ 2, because Net Income is a period measure but Total Assets is a point-in-time balance — the timeframes must match.
  • Higher ROA = more profitable/efficient use of assets.

Journal Entry Examples

Chapter 1 uses accounting-equation transaction analysis (not formal debits/credits yet). Worked nine-transaction example from the lecture: Smart Touch Learning, investor Sheena Bright. Each row shows the effect on A = L + SE (equation stays in balance after every transaction):

# Transaction Assets = Liab. + Equity
1 Owner contributes $30,000 cash for common stock Cash +30,000 — Common Stock +30,000
2 Buy land for $20,000 cash Cash −20,000, Land +20,000 — —
3 Buy $500 supplies on account Supplies +500 A/P +500 —
4 Earn $5,500 service revenue for cash Cash +5,500 — Service Revenue (R/E) +5,500
5 Earn $3,000 service revenue on account A/R +3,000 — Service Revenue (R/E) +3,000
6 Pay $3,200 cash expenses ($2,000 rent + $1,200 salaries) Cash −3,200 — Rent + Salaries Exp (R/E) −3,200
7 Pay $300 on account (toward Txn 3) Cash −300 A/P −300 —
8 Collect $2,000 on account (from Txn 5) Cash +2,000, A/R −2,000 — — (NOT new revenue)
9 Pay $5,000 cash dividend Cash −5,000 — Dividends (R/E) −5,000

Notes from the lecture:

  • Txn 2 & 8 are shifts within assets — total assets unchanged.
  • Txn 5 (revenue on account): revenue is recognized when the service is performed, not when cash is received.
  • Txn 8 (collection): cash collected from a prior receivable is not revenue — that was already recorded in Txn 5.
  • Txn 9 (dividend): reduces R/E but is not an expense.

If your section uses formal entries, the equivalent debit/credit form of Txn 1 is Dr. Cash 30,000 / Cr. Common Stock 30,000, but Chapter 1 lectures stay at the equation level.

Common Mistakes

  • Analyzing from the wrong perspective. Always analyze from the company's viewpoint, not your own. When Sheena Bright contributes cash, the company receives cash and issues stock — don't think "she lost money."
  • Confusing cash collection with revenue (the professor's #1 stressed mistake). Collecting cash on a receivable (Txn 8) is NOT new revenue — revenue was recorded when the service was performed (Txn 5). Cash ≠ Revenue.
  • Treating dividends as an expense. Dividends are a distribution of earnings to owners, not an expense. They do not appear on the Income Statement — they go on the Statement of Retained Earnings.
  • Forgetting the equation must always balance. Every transaction keeps A = L + SE in balance; if it doesn't, you missed a side.
  • Mixing up "for a period" vs. "as of a date." Only the Balance Sheet is "as of"; the other three are "for the period."
  • Liability/tax confusion across business forms. Sole prop & partnership = unlimited liability; corporation & LLC = limited. Corporation = double taxation; LLC avoids it (pass-through). Don't swap these.
  • Using total assets instead of average total assets in ROA. You must average beginning and ending to match Net Income's period.
  • Recording immeasurable items. A great CEO's skills can't be an asset (Monetary Unit Assumption).
  • Mixing owner and company transactions (violates the Economic Entity Assumption).

Exam Checklist

  • ☐ Define accounting as the "language of business"; distinguish financial (external users) vs. managerial (internal users) accounting.
  • ☐ Name the two key external users — creditors (interest + principal) and investors (ownership, stock gains, dividends) — and what each wants.
  • ☐ List the two primary qualitative characteristics (Relevance, Faithful Representation) and their sub-components; know materiality (the $10-in-a-billion example).
  • ☐ List the four enhancing characteristics (Comparability, Verifiability, Timeliness, Understandability); know Comparability ↔ GAAP and Consistency ↔ same company over time.
  • ☐ Know the SEC → FASB → GAAP chain and that IASB → IFRS (international).
  • ☐ Know SOX (2002): triggered by Enron, created the PCAOB, strengthened auditor independence (Arthur Andersen conflict of interest).
  • ☐ State the four assumptions (Monetary Unit, Economic Entity, Periodicity, Going Concern) with an example of each.
  • ☐ Compare the four business forms on owners, liability, taxation (double taxation vs. pass-through), and life. Know LLC = hybrid.
  • ☐ Explain corporate structure: stockholders → vote → Board of Directors → appoints CEO/CFO.
  • ☐ Write the accounting equation and explain left (uses) vs. right (sources: creditors = liabilities, investors = equity).
  • ☐ Apply "receivable" = asset, "payable" = liability, "on account" = credit rules.

  • ☐ Distinguish "for a period" (IS, R/E, SCF) vs. "as of a date" (Balance Sheet).

Chapter 2 — Recording Business Transactions

A fast review for the Ch 1–2 exam. Built directly from the lecture videos (the professor's own explanations and examples), with the textbook used only to confirm terms and structure. This chapter is the foundation for everything that follows — if you can classify accounts and apply debit/credit rules quickly, the rest of the course gets much easier.

Key Concepts

  • Account — a detailed record of all increases and decreases in one specific item (e.g., Cash). The accounting equation (A = L + SE) is broken down into individual accounts.
  • Chart of Accounts vs. Ledger — the professor stresses this distinction. The Chart of Accounts is just a categorized list of account names (a reference list). The Ledger is the actual collection of all T-accounts with their balances.
  • T-Account — a visual of one ledger account. Left = Debit (Dr.), Right = Credit (Cr.) — the professor says repeatedly: "do not overcomplicate this; left simply means debit, right simply means credit." Forget your personal bank/credit-card meaning of "credit."
  • Normal Balance — the side on which an account increases. Derived from where the account sits in the equation: left side of equation (Assets) → debit normal balance; right side (Liabilities + Equity) → credit normal balance.
  • Double-Entry System — every transaction affects at least two accounts, with at least one debit and one credit. Total debits must always equal total credits.
  • Transaction analysis → journalize → post → trial balance — the chronological sequence the professor wants you to know cold. A transaction is an event that affects the company's financial position; each one starts from a source document (invoice, check, billing statement).
  • Journal — a chronological record of transactions.
  • Ledger / Posting — posting = transferring the dollar amounts from the journal to the matching T-accounts. Debits go to the debit side, credits go to the credit side.
  • Ending balance — found by netting total debits vs. total credits in a T-account; it sits on the side of the account's normal balance (larger side wins).
  • Trial Balance — a list of all accounts and their ending balances in debit/credit columns. Purpose: check the mathematical equality of total debits and total credits. It is an internal working document, NOT a Balance Sheet (it still contains revenues, expenses, and dividends). Order: Assets → Liabilities → Stockholders' Equity.
  • Two key "becomes later" accounts the professor highlights:
  • Prepaid Expense = ASSET. Any item with the prefix "prepaid" (prepaid rent, prepaid insurance) is an asset — you paid cash in advance, you haven't used it yet, so you have a right to it. It becomes an expense only when consumed. (Office Supplies works the same way but gets its own Supplies account.)
  • Unearned Revenue = LIABILITY. The mirror image of a prepaid expense: cash received before the service is performed, so the company owes a future service. It becomes revenue only when the service is delivered.
  • Debt Ratio = Total Liabilities ÷ Total Assets — the chapter's featured ratio. Higher = more assets financed by debt (more interest pressure), but a high ratio isn't automatically bad if cash flow can cover it. Never judge a company on one ratio.

Key Formulas & Rules

Expanded accounting equation (how equity breaks down):

Assets = Liabilities + Stockholders' Equity where Stockholders' Equity = Common Stock + Retained Earnings and Retained Earnings is affected by: + Revenues − Expenses − Dividends

  • Contributed Capital = Common Stock. Earned Capital = Retained Earnings.
  • Important correction the professor makes on video: Dividends are NOT part of Contributed Capital. Dividends are a reduction of Retained Earnings (Earned Capital).

The Golden Rule: for every journal entry, total Debits = total Credits. This holds even for compound entries (multiple debits and/or credits). If it balances, the accounting equation stays in balance.

Debit/Credit rules and normal balances by account type (memorize this — it was drilled on the timed Ch1–2 practice test):

Account Type Normal Balance Increase with Decrease with
Assets Debit Debit Credit
Liabilities Credit Credit Debit
Common Stock Credit Credit Debit
Retained Earnings Credit Credit Debit
Revenues Credit Credit Debit
Expenses Debit Debit Credit
Dividends Debit Debit Credit

DEALER memory aid — accounts with a normal DEBIT balance:

  • Dividends, Expenses, Assets → normal balance Debit (increase with a debit)
  • Liabilities, Equity (Common Stock + Retained Earnings), Revenues → normal balance Credit (increase with a credit)

The professor's logic chain (why Expenses & Dividends are debits — a favorite exam trap):

  • Equity has a credit normal balance → Revenue increases equity, so Revenue is a credit.
  • Expenses and Dividends decrease Retained Earnings → a decrease to a credit-balance account is recorded on the opposite side → therefore Expenses and Dividends have debit normal balances.

Tip from the videos: do not put + / − signs inside T-accounts. The side (left/right) already tells you debit vs. credit; +/− just creates confusion.

Common Mistakes

  • Treating "credit" like a bank/credit-card. In accounting, debit/credit are just left/right directional labels — nothing to do with good/bad or money in/out.
  • Mixing up Chart of Accounts and Ledger. Chart = list of names only; Ledger = T-accounts with balances.
  • Getting Expenses/Dividends backwards. They have debit normal balances because they decrease equity. Students often credit them by accident.
  • Calling Dividends "Contributed Capital." The professor explicitly corrects this: Dividends reduce Earned Capital (Retained Earnings).
  • Recording cash received for future service as Revenue. It's Unearned Service Revenue (a liability) until the service is performed.
  • Recording collection of A/R as Revenue again. (Apr 25) Revenue was already earned when the service was performed; collecting cash only swaps one asset (A/R) for another (Cash).
  • Forgetting "on account" has two directions. Owed to you = Accounts Receivable (asset); owed by you = Accounts Payable (liability).
  • Putting +/− signs inside T-accounts — leads to errors; use only Dr./Cr. position.
  • Writing the credit account first / not indenting it. Debit always on top; credit indented below.
  • Thinking a balanced trial balance = no errors. It still misses wrong-account postings or equal wrong amounts on both sides.
  • Confusing the Trial Balance with the Balance Sheet. The trial balance still lists revenues, expenses, and dividends; the Balance Sheet does not.

Exam Checklist

  • ☐ Recite the DEALER rule instantly: Dividends, Expenses, Assets = debit; Liabilities, Equity, Revenue = credit.
  • ☐ State the normal balance of any account on sight (the timed practice test demands speed).
  • ☐ Know increase/decrease → debit or credit for all 7 account types (table above).
  • ☐ Write the expanded equation and place RE = + Rev − Exp − Div.
  • ☐ Classify the tricky accounts: Prepaid = asset, Unearned Revenue = liability, Supplies = asset, Dividends reduce Retained Earnings.
  • ☐ Journalize all common transaction types (cash sale, sale on account, purchase on account, payment on account, collection on account, expenses paid in cash, accrued bill, unearned revenue, dividends, borrowing, buying an asset).
  • ☐ Format every entry: date, debit on top, credit indented, debits = credits.
  • ☐ Build a compound entry (multiple debits/credits) and confirm it balances.
  • ☐ Post to T-accounts and compute an ending balance (net larger side − smaller side; result sits on normal-balance side).
  • ☐ Prepare a trial balance in order Assets → Liabilities → Equity; verify totals match.
  • ☐ Explain why the trial balance is not a Balance Sheet, and that a balanced TB ≠ error-free.
  • ☐ Know the three error-finding methods: search for missing account; ÷ 2 (debit/credit reversed); ÷ 9 (slide or transposition error).
  • ☐ State the financial-statement order: Income Statement → Statement of Retained Earnings → Balance Sheet.
  • ☐ Compute and interpret the Debt Ratio = Total Liabilities ÷ Total Assets.
