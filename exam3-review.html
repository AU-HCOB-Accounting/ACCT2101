Chapter 5 — Merchandising Operations

Key Concepts

  • Merchandiser vs. manufacturer. A merchandiser does NOT make goods — it buys finished goods and resells them at a higher price. Wholesalers (sell to retailers) and retailers (sell to consumers) are both merchandisers. Profit model: buy at $70, sell at $100 → $30 gross profit.
  • Merchandise Inventory. The asset account for goods bought to resell. A permanent (asset) account with a beginning balance. A service company has no such account; a merchandiser reports it on the balance sheet.
  • Every sale = TWO journal entries. (1) Record the revenue (Cash/AR ↔ Sales Revenue) at the sales price, and (2) record Cost of Goods Sold ↔ Merchandise Inventory at the cost. This is the single most repeated point in the lectures.
  • COGS behaves like a prepaid expense (professor's analogy). Cash is paid up front, the item is capitalized as an asset, and it becomes an expense only when consumed/sold. Same pattern as Equipment (→ Depreciation Exp.) and Supplies (→ Supplies Exp.). For inventory: asset = Merchandise Inventory, expense = COGS. The $70 sits as an asset until the moment of sale.
  • Multi-step income statement. Sales Revenue − COGS = Gross Profit; − Operating Expenses (salaries, rent, advertising, depreciation, admin) = Operating Income; ± Other Income & Expenses = Income Before Taxes; − Tax Expense = Net Income. COGS sits immediately below Sales Revenue. (Single-step lumps all revenues then all expenses; rarely used by big public companies.)
  • Net Sales Revenue = gross Sales Revenue − Sales Returns & Allowances − Sales Discounts (parallel to Net Purchases).
  • Credit terms (e.g., 2/10, n/30). "2% discount if paid within 10 days; otherwise full net amount due in 30 days." Offered by the seller to incentivize prompt payment. (PPT also uses 3/15, n/30.)
  • Gross vs. Net method — what to use: Record purchases using the GROSS method and sales using the GROSS method as well. ⚠️ The video lectures recorded sales using the Net method (they were made with the previous-edition PPT), but per the Chapter 5 Additional Study Note, the Gross method for sales is used in ALL assignments and exams, unless otherwise instructed. This summary therefore shows sales under the Gross method. (The Net method for sales is appendix material — Appendix 5B / LO 5.8.)
  • FOB Shipping Point vs. FOB Destination. Determines who owns goods in transit (whose balance sheet on Dec. 31) and who pays freight:
  • FOB Shipping Point — title passes to buyer when goods leave the seller (loaded on carrier). Goods in transit on 12/31 belong to the buyer. Buyer pays freight = Freight-in.
  • FOB Destination — title passes when goods arrive at buyer. Goods in transit on 12/31 belong to the seller. Seller pays freight = Freight-out.
  • Freight-in is capitalized into inventory (cost of an asset = all costs to get it ready for its intended use). It becomes expense later through COGS. Freight-out is an operating (Delivery) Expense for the seller.
  • Sales Returns & Allowances need TWO timelines (unlike Purchase R&A, which is one entry): (1) a year-end estimating adjusting entry (revenue recognition standard — report only revenue you expect to keep), then (2) entries when the actual return happens. The professor's example: sell $100 but 60% historically comes back → can't report the full $100.
  • Allowance = price reduction with NO physical goods returned. Therefore allowances touch only the revenue/refund side — no inventory or COGS entry.
  • Actual returns have NO income-statement effect — revenue and COGS were already reversed in the estimating entry; touching them again would double-count. Actual return just clears the balance-sheet accounts (Refunds Payable, Estimated Returns Inventory).
  • Sales Discount (Gross method — the tested method) = a contra-revenue account (normal debit balance) recorded when the customer pays within the discount window; it is subtracted in computing Net Sales Revenue. (Under the Net method shown in the videos, the parallel account is Sales Discounts Forfeited — an other-income account recorded only when the customer pays after the window, and it is NOT part of Net Sales. Net method is appendix-only for this course.)
  • Inventory shrinkage. Physical count < book balance (theft, damage, errors). Even with a perpetual system, a physical count is still done. Treated like a sale: Dr. COGS / Cr. Merchandise Inventory.
  • Perpetual vs. Periodic. This course uses Perpetual only (continuous running record; COGS recorded at every sale). Periodic uses a temporary "Purchases" account and computes COGS only at period-end via physical count — appendix material, not tested.
  • Gross Profit Percentage = Gross Profit ÷ Net Sales Revenue — measures profit earned per sales dollar above cost.

Key Formulas & Rules

  • Net Purchases = Purchases − Purchase Returns & Allowances − Purchase Discounts (Freight-in is NOT part of this figure, though it is added to inventory cost).
  • Cost of Goods Available for Sale = Beginning Inventory + Net Purchases + Freight-in.

  • COGS equation (MEMORIZE — professor stressed this): Beginning Inventory + Net Purchases + Freight-in = COGS + Ending Inventory

  • Net Sales Revenue = Sales Revenue − Sales Returns & Allowances − Sales Discounts.

  • Gross Profit = Net Sales Revenue − COGS.
  • Gross Profit % = Gross Profit ÷ Net Sales Revenue.
  • Discount-after-return rule: the cash discount is computed on the AP balance after returns, not the original. (e.g., $100 purchase − $20 return = $80; discount applies to $80.)
  • Freight is never discountable — purchase discounts apply only to the merchandise cost, never to freight charges.
  • Pricing rule: Sales Revenue is recorded at the selling price; COGS at the original cost. Always two different numbers.

Journal Entry Examples

(Perpetual system. Purchases = GROSS method; Sales = GROSS method — the method used in all assignments and exams per the Chapter 5 Additional Study Note.)

Purchase on account Dr. Merchandise Inventory 100 / Cr. Accounts Payable 100

Pay WITHIN discount period (2/10, n/30; $100, take $2 discount) Dr. Accounts Payable 100 / Cr. Cash 98 / Cr. Merchandise Inventory 2 (discount reduces the asset's cost basis to $98)

Pay AFTER discount period Dr. Accounts Payable 100 / Cr. Cash 100 (inventory stays at $100)

Purchase return or allowance Dr. Accounts Payable XX / Cr. Merchandise Inventory XX

Freight-in (buyer pays, FOB Shipping Point) Dr. Merchandise Inventory XX / Cr. Cash XX

Freight-out (seller pays, FOB Destination) Dr. Delivery Expense (Freight-out) XX / Cr. Cash XX

Sale on account — gross method ($100, 2/10 n/30; cost $70) — two entries:

  • Entry 1 (revenue at gross): Dr. Accounts Receivable 100 / Cr. Sales Revenue 100
  • Entry 2 (cost): Dr. Cost of Goods Sold 70 / Cr. Merchandise Inventory 70

Collect WITHIN discount period (customer takes the 2% discount) Dr. Cash 98 / Dr. Sales Discount 2 / Cr. Accounts Receivable 100

Collect AFTER discount period (no discount taken) Dr. Cash 100 / Cr. Accounts Receivable 100

(Net method — videos/old PPT, appendix only, NOT used on assignments/exams: record the sale at net $98; if the customer pays late, Dr. Cash 100 / Cr. Accounts Receivable 98 / Cr. Sales Discounts Forfeited 2.)

Year-end ESTIMATE of sales returns (adjusting entry) — two entries: Revenue side: Dr. Sales Revenue XX / Cr. Refunds Payable XX Cost side: Dr. Estimated Returns Inventory XX / Cr. Cost of Goods Sold XX

Actual return occurs next period (NO income-statement accounts) — two entries: Dr. Refunds Payable XX / Cr. Cash (or A/R) XX Dr. Merchandise Inventory XX / Cr. Estimated Returns Inventory XX

Year-end ESTIMATE of sales allowance (no goods returning — revenue side only): Dr. Sales Revenue XX / Cr. Refunds Payable XX

Actual sales allowance granted (price cut, no inventory): Dr. Refunds Payable XX / Cr. Cash (or A/R) XX

Inventory shrinkage (physical count < books) Dr. Cost of Goods Sold XX / Cr. Merchandise Inventory XX

Common Mistakes

  • Forgetting the second (COGS) entry on a sale. Every sale = two entries. Recording only the revenue is the classic error.
  • Mixing up sales price vs. cost. Revenue uses the selling price; COGS uses the original cost — never the same number.
  • Applying the discount to the wrong base after a return. Compute the discount on the AP balance after the return, not the original invoice.
  • Discounting freight. Freight charges are never eligible for the purchase discount.
  • Confusing Freight-in with Freight-out. Freight-in → asset (Merchandise Inventory), buyer, FOB Shipping Point. Freight-out → operating expense (Delivery Expense), seller, FOB Destination.
  • Wrong owner of goods in transit on 12/31. FOB Shipping Point → buyer's books; FOB Destination → seller's books.
  • Recording revenue/COGS again on an actual return → double-counts. Actual returns only clear Refunds Payable and Estimated Returns Inventory.
  • Adding a cost-side entry to an allowance. Allowances = no physical goods back → revenue/refund side only.
  • Crediting purchase discount to revenue/income. It is credited to Merchandise Inventory (reduces cost basis), not treated as income.
  • Treating "Purchases" as an inventory account. Under perpetual (this course), debit Merchandise Inventory directly. "Purchases" is a periodic-system temporary account (not tested).
  • Mishandling the sales discount. Under the tested Gross method, record the full $100 at the sale and debit Sales Discount (contra-revenue) only if the customer pays within the window. Don't use the Net-method Sales Discounts Forfeited account on assignments/exams — that's appendix-only.
  • Forgetting Freight-in in the COGS equation while leaving it out of Net Purchases — it's added to Goods Available for Sale even though it isn't part of "Net Purchases."

Exam Checklist

  • ☐ Record a sale with both entries (revenue at price; COGS at cost).
  • ☐ Purchase entries using the GROSS method; pay within/after discount period correctly (discount → Cr. Merchandise Inventory).
  • ☐ Sales entries using the GROSS method (record A/R & Sales at the full price; if paid within the window → Dr. Sales Discount). This is the method used on all assignments and exams.
  • ☐ Compute purchase discount on the post-return AP balance; remember freight is not discountable.
  • ☐ Classify freight: Freight-in → Merchandise Inventory; Freight-out → Delivery Expense.
  • ☐ Determine ownership of goods in transit by FOB term (Shipping Point = buyer; Destination = seller).
  • ☐ Sales Returns & Allowances: write the year-end estimate (Refunds Payable / Estimated Returns Inventory) AND the actual entries — and know actuals have no I/S effect.
  • ☐ Distinguish a return (goods come back → inventory + COGS entries) from an allowance (no goods → revenue side only).
  • ☐ Record inventory shrinkage: Dr. COGS / Cr. Merchandise Inventory.
  • ☐ Build a multi-step income statement: Net Sales − COGS = Gross Profit − Op. Exp. = Operating Income ± Other = Pre-tax − Tax = Net Income.
  • ☐ Apply the COGS equation: Beg. Inv. + Net Purchases + Freight-in = COGS + End. Inv.
  • ☐ Compute Gross Profit % = Gross Profit ÷ Net Sales Revenue.
  • ☐ Use the Perpetual system throughout (periodic is appendix/not tested).

Chapter 6 — Merchandise Inventory

Key Concepts

The core problem. Recording inventory purchases and sales is easy; the hard part is figuring out Cost of Goods Sold (COGS) when you buy identical units at different prices over time. Sales Revenue is always units × selling price (the professor fixes selling price at $100). But which cost — $70, $75, or $80 — do you assign to a unit that left the warehouse? That is the entire chapter.

Two simple cases first. Buy 1 unit @ $70, sell @ $100 → COGS = $70. Buy 3 units @ $70, sell all 3 → COGS = $210. As long as the unit cost never changes, COGS is trivial. The professor stresses: in real life, prices rise over time (inflation), so costs do change.

Specific Identification. When you have a few unique, high-value items (cars, jewelry, real estate), you physically know exactly which unit was sold, so you use that unit's actual cost. Specific ID is NOT a cost flow assumption — no assumption is needed because the real cost is known.

Cost flow assumptions. When you have hundreds/thousands of identical, mixed units, you can't track which physical unit sold. So accountants use logical approximations. The three cost flow assumptions: FIFO, LIFO, Weighted-Average. Key emphasis: these describe the flow of costs, not the physical flow of goods.

  • FIFO (First-In, First-Out): oldest costs → COGS; newest costs stay in Ending Inventory.
  • LIFO (Last-In, First-Out): newest costs → COGS; oldest costs stay in Ending Inventory.
  • Weighted-Average: blend all costs into one average unit cost, used for both COGS and Ending Inventory. (Perpetual version = "moving average" — recompute after every purchase, never after a sale.)

Perpetual vs. Periodic (LO 6.7 = appendix, NOT tested, but know the logic):

  • Perpetual: compute COGS at the moment of each sale; running inventory balance always current.
  • Periodic: record only revenue at sale (no COGS entry); wait until period-end, do a physical count, then compute COGS for the whole period at once.

Effects when costs are RISING (the professor's standard assumption):

Method COGS Gross Profit / Net Income Ending Inventory Income Tax
FIFO Lowest Highest Highest (newest costs) Highest
Weighted-Avg Middle Middle Middle Middle
LIFO Highest Lowest Lowest (oldest costs) Lowest

Why FIFO prefers? Higher profit to investors/lenders. Why LIFO prefers? Lower net income → lower income tax (real cash savings). FIFO ending inventory best reflects current replacement cost.

Four accounting principles for inventory (LO 6.1):

  • Consistency — use the same method period to period. Can't flip LIFO→FIFO just to boost earnings; changes are rare and require justification in the notes.
  • Disclosure — must state in the notes which costing method (FIFO/LIFO/etc.) was used.
  • Materiality — apply strict inventory accounting only to significant items. Don't track ten $1 pencils as inventory; expense them as supplies.
  • Conservatism — "anticipate no gains, provide for all probable losses." Record assets at lowest reasonable amount, liabilities at highest. Underlies the LCM/LCNRV rule.

Key Formulas & Rules

  • Goods Available for Sale = Beginning Inventory + Net Purchases
  • Cost of Goods Available for Sale − Ending Inventory = COGS
  • Goods Available for Sale − COGS = Ending Inventory (rearranged; use to verify any method)
  • Average Unit Cost = Total Cost of Goods Available for Sale ÷ Total Units Available
  • Inventory Turnover = COGS ÷ Average Merchandise Inventory, where Average Inventory = (Beginning + Ending) ÷ 2. Higher = sells faster / more efficient.
  • Days' Sales in Inventory = 365 ÷ Inventory Turnover. Lower = sells faster. (Prof's example: COGS $100, avg inv $10 → turnover 10×; 365 ÷ 10 = 36.5 days.)
  • LCM / LCNRV rule: report inventory at the lower of cost vs. market. FIFO/weighted-avg → lower of cost and NRV (selling price − costs to complete & sell). LIFO/retail → traditional LCM with replacement cost. Write down only if market < cost; never write up (conservatism).

Journal Entry Examples

Purchase of inventory:

Dr. Merchandise Inventory      XXX
   Cr. Cash / Accounts Payable     XXX

Sale of inventory (always TWO entries):

Dr. Accounts Receivable        100   (selling price)
   Cr. Sales Revenue               100

Dr. Cost of Goods Sold          70   (cost — the costing method sets this number)
   Cr. Merchandise Inventory        70

The JE format is identical under FIFO, LIFO, and Weighted-Avg — only the COGS/Inventory dollar amount changes.

LCM / LCNRV write-down (cost $3,000, market falls to $2,200 → write down $800; prof's big example: $20,000 cost vs $10,000 market → write down $10,000):

Dr. Cost of Goods Sold         800
   Cr. Merchandise Inventory       800

Recognize the decline as expense immediately, even though no sale occurred. If market rises above cost → no entry (don't write up).

Worked cost-flow computations — Conceptual example (650 units available; 100@$70, 250@$75, 300@$80 = $49,750; sell 400; selling price $100 → Sales = $40,000):

  • FIFO COGS = (100×$70)+(250×$75)+(50×$80) = $7,000+$18,750+$4,000 = $29,750. Ending Inv = 250×$80 = $20,000. Gross Profit = $40,000−$29,750 = $10,250.
  • LIFO COGS = (300×$80)+(100×$75) = $24,000+$7,500 = $31,500. Ending Inv = (100×$70)+(150×$75) = $7,000+$11,250 = $18,250. Gross Profit = $8,500.
  • Weighted-Avg: $49,750 ÷ 650 ≈ $76.54/unit. COGS = 400×$76.54 ≈ $30,615. Ending Inv = 250×$76.54 ≈ $19,135. Gross Profit = $9,385.

Pattern check: FIFO lowest COGS / highest profit / highest ending inv; LIFO opposite; Avg in middle — a mathematical result of rising costs.

Worked perpetual example (Beg 2@$350=$700; Aug 5 buy 4@$360; Aug 15 sell 4; Aug 26 buy 12@$380; Aug 31 sell 10. GAFS = $700 + $6,000 = $6,700):

  • Perpetual FIFO: Aug 15 COGS = (2×$350)+(2×$360) = $1,420; Aug 31 COGS = (2×$360)+(8×$380) = $3,760. Total COGS = $5,180; Ending Inv = 4×$380 = $1,520. ($5,180 + $1,520 = $6,700 ✓)
  • Perpetual LIFO: Aug 15 COGS = 4×$360 = $1,440; Aug 31 COGS = 10×$380 = $3,800. Total COGS = $5,240; Ending Inv = (2×$350)+(2×$380) = $1,460. ($5,240 + $1,460 = $6,700 ✓)
  • Moving Average: after Aug 5 → $2,140 ÷ 6 = $356.67; Aug 15 COGS = 4×$356.67 ≈ $1,427 (2 left = $713). After Aug 26 → ($713+$4,560) ÷ 14 = $376.64; Aug 31 COGS = 10×$376.64 ≈ $3,766; Ending Inv = 4×$376.64 ≈ $1,507.

Inventory errors (LO 6.5): An ending-inventory error ripples through COGS, Gross Profit, Net Income.

  • Ending Inv understated → COGS overstated → Gross Profit/Net Income understated.
  • Ending Inv overstated → COGS understated → Gross Profit/Net Income overstated.
  • Key: Period 1 ending inventory = Period 2 beginning inventory, so the error reverses next period. Over two periods total net income is correct, but each year's statements are individually misstated.

Common Mistakes

  • Confusing physical flow with cost flow. LIFO doesn't mean you literally ship the newest goods; it's a cost assumption only.
  • Calling Specific Identification a cost flow assumption. It isn't — actual costs are known.
  • Recording only one entry on a sale. A sale needs TWO: revenue entry AND the COGS/inventory entry.
  • Mixing up which method gives highest profit. When costs rise: FIFO = highest profit + highest ending inventory; LIFO = lowest of both. (If costs fall, the pattern flips.)
  • Writing inventory UP under LCM. You only write down; never up (would anticipate a gain — violates conservatism).
  • Forgetting moving-average recalculates only after a PURCHASE, not after a sale.
  • Forgetting the Period-1-ending = Period-2-beginning link when analyzing inventory errors.
  • Using the wrong "average" in turnover — it's (Beg + End) ÷ 2, not just ending inventory.
  • Periodic vs. perpetual timing: periodic records NO COGS at sale time; COGS computed once at period-end after physical count.

Exam Checklist

  • ☐ Record purchase and the two-part sale journal entries; know Sales = price, COGS = cost.
  • ☐ Compute COGS and Ending Inventory under FIFO, LIFO, and Weighted-Average (perpetual). Practice the perpetual schedule until automatic.
  • ☐ Moving average: recompute average unit cost after each purchase only.
  • ☐ Verify with GAFS = COGS + Ending Inventory (must reconcile).
  • ☐ State the rising-cost effects table from memory (FIFO highest profit/EI; LIFO lowest; tax opposite).
  • ☐ Explain why FIFO/LIFO produce their results (cheap old costs vs. expensive new costs).
  • ☐ Apply LCM/LCNRV: pick lower of cost vs. market; write-down JE = Dr. COGS / Cr. Merchandise Inventory; no write-ups.
  • ☐ Trace an inventory error through COGS → Gross Profit → Net Income for both periods; know it self-corrects after two years.
  • ☐ Compute Inventory Turnover and Days' Sales in Inventory; interpret high vs. low.
  • ☐ Know the four principles (Consistency, Disclosure, Materiality, Conservatism).
  • ☐ Know perpetual vs. periodic concept (periodic appendix NOT tested, per professor).

Chapter 7 — Internal Control and Cash

Exam-prep summary. The professor was explicit: he is selective. He skipped petty cash and credit card sales (L.O. 4 & 5) — he said he will NOT test or assign homework on skipped topics. The lecture's overwhelming focus is L.O. 6: Bank Reconciliation. Study that hard.

Key Concepts

Internal control (L.O. 1, definition only): The organizational plan and all related measures adopted by an entity to (1) safeguard assets, (2) encourage employees to follow company policies, (3) promote operational efficiency, and (4) ensure accurate and reliable accounting records. Know this 4-part definition.

L.O. 2 & 3 (cash receipts / cash payments): Professor said these are "very straightforward — read once and you'll get it." Just know the controls exist to safeguard cash (the most liquid, easiest-to-steal asset).

Why cash matters: Cash is arguably the most important asset because the ultimate goal of operations is to earn cash. So we safeguard it (deposit in a bank, don't keep in a vault) and record it accurately.

Bank account as a control device: Depositing cash creates a double record — one in the company's books, one at the bank — giving an independent check. Comparing the two is the bank reconciliation.

What a bank reconciliation is: After all entries are posted, the company's ending Cash balance (per books) should equal the bank statement's ending balance, but they almost never match. Two reasons:

  1. Timing differences (e.g., employee deposits cash after the bank closed on Dec 31 → company recorded it, bank hasn't = a deposit in transit).
  2. Errors (can be on the company's side OR the bank's side).

Two sides — the governing logic (memorize this):

  • Bank side = adjust for items the company already recorded correctly/timely, but the bank hasn't processed yet (pure timing). The company's books are already right → NO journal entry needed.
  • Book side = adjust for items the bank already processed, but the company hasn't recorded yet (company didn't know until the statement arrived) → journal entries REQUIRED.
  • Quick test: "Who already knows about this transaction?" Company knows only → Bank side. Bank knows only → Book side. Errors go on the side that made the mistake.

Debit/Credit Memorandums (from the BANK's perspective): To the bank, your account is a liability (they owe you). So:

  • Credit Memorandum = bank increased your account (good for you): note collections, interest earned, EFT receipts → ADD on Book side.
  • Debit Memorandum = bank decreased your account (bad for you): service charges, NSF checks → SUBTRACT on Book side.

Key Formulas & Rules

Bank reconciliation structure (both sides must reach the SAME corrected/"true" cash balance):

BANK SIDE (no journal entries) BOOK SIDE (journal entries required)
Start: Balance per bank statement Start: Balance per books (Cash ledger)
+ Deposits in transit (outstanding deposits) + Bank collections (notes receivable + interest)
− Outstanding checks + EFT receipts (customer pays you electronically)
+/− Bank errors + Interest revenue earned on account
− Service charges
− NSF (bounced) checks
− EFT payments (you pay a bill electronically)
+/− Book errors
= Adjusted bank balance = Adjusted book balance

CRITICAL RULE: Only the Book side requires journal entries. Writing "+1,035" or "−30" on the reconciliation sheet does NOT update the general ledger — you must journalize each Book-side item.

NSF check rule: A bounced check ≠ the customer is off the hook. Subtract it from cash AND reinstate it as an Account Receivable (customer still owes you).

Book error rule (transposition example): Wrote Check No. 443 for $1,226; bank processed $1,226 correctly; accountant recorded $1,262 in the books. You subtracted $36 too much → ADD $36 back to the book balance.

Laird Company numbers (professor's worked example):

  • Bank side: $15,907.45 + 2,201.40 (dep. in transit) − 5,904.00 (outstanding chks) = $12,204.85
  • Book side: $11,589.45 + 1,035.00 (note) + 36.00 (error) − 425.60 (NSF) − 30.00 (svc chg) = $12,204.85 ✓

Journal Entry Examples

(Only Book-side items — these are the four Laird Company entries.)

1. Collection of note receivable ($1,000 note + $50 interest − $15 collection fee = $1,035 net):

Dr. Cash                    1,035
Dr. Miscellaneous Expense      15
    Cr. Notes Receivable           1,000
    Cr. Interest Revenue              50

2. Book error correction (Check No. 443; $36 oversubtracted from a payment on Accounts Payable):

Dr. Cash                       36
    Cr. Accounts Payable           36

3. NSF check ($425.60 bounced — remove fake cash, reinstate the receivable):

Dr. Accounts Receivable    425.60
    Cr. Cash                   425.60

4. Bank service charge ($30):

Dr. Miscellaneous Expense      30
    Cr. Cash                       30

Verification: Post all four to the Cash T-account → $11,589.45 + 1,035 + 36 − 425.60 − 30 = $12,204.85, matching the adjusted bank balance.

Common Mistakes

  • Journalizing Bank-side items. Deposits in transit and outstanding checks were ALREADY recorded by the company — re-recording double-counts. No entry.
  • Forgetting that ONLY the Book side needs entries. This is the #1 tested distinction.
  • Putting an item on the wrong side. Use "Who already knows?" If unsure on a memo: Credit memo → add on Book; Debit memo → subtract on Book.
  • Confusing debit/credit memo direction. They're from the bank's view: credit memo = MORE cash for you; debit memo = LESS cash for you (opposite of your own debit/credit intuition).
  • NSF check → just reducing cash. You must also debit Accounts Receivable — the customer still owes you.
  • Book error direction. Decide whether the error overstated or understated cash. Recorded payment too high ($1,262 vs $1,226) → you removed too much cash → ADD it back.
  • Putting a bank error on the Book side (or vice versa). Error goes on the side of whoever made the mistake.
  • Reporting the wrong cash figure on the balance sheet. Report the adjusted (true) balance, not the raw book or bank number.

Exam Checklist

  • ☐ State the 4-part internal control definition (safeguard assets, encourage policy compliance, promote efficiency, ensure reliable records).
  • ☐ Explain why book and bank balances differ: timing differences + errors.
  • ☐ Build a full reconciliation: correct starting point, correct items on each side, both sides tie to the same true cash balance.
  • ☐ Bank side: + deposits in transit, − outstanding checks, +/− bank errors → NO journal entries.
  • ☐ Book side: + collections/EFT receipts/interest, − service charges/NSF/EFT payments, +/− book errors → journal entries REQUIRED.
  • ☐ Know which reconciling item triggers an adjusting entry (answer: Book-side items, e.g., service charges, NSF, collections — NOT outstanding checks, deposits in transit, or bank errors).
  • ☐ Journalize the four classic Book-side entries (note collection, book error, NSF, service charge) from memory.
  • ☐ Credit memo = add (Book side); Debit memo = subtract (Book side) — from the bank's perspective.
  • ☐ NSF check: Dr. Accounts Receivable / Cr. Cash (reinstate the customer's debt).
  • ☐ Confirm the adjusted Cash T-account balance equals the adjusted bank balance.
  • ☐ (Skipped — not tested: petty cash, credit card sales journalizing.)

Chapter 8 — Receivables

Key Concepts

  • What is a receivable? A right to receive cash created whenever a business sells goods/services on account (on credit). The seller is the creditor (receives the receivable); the buyer is the debtor (must pay later).
  • Two main types (know the differences cold — the professor stressed this):
  • Accounts Receivable (A/R) — "trade receivables." Due in 30–60 days, no formal written document, usually does NOT bear interest (companies may instead offer early-payment discount terms). Current asset.
  • Notes Receivable (N/R) — "promissory notes." Longer term, evidenced by a legally binding written document, and bears interest (principal + interest by the maturity date).
  • Other receivables = anything else (dividends, interest, taxes receivable).
  • Two N/R scenarios: (1) an existing A/R is converted into an N/R when the customer can't pay on time and "buys more time" by signing a note; or (2) a note is received directly from a sale.
  • Why estimate uncollectibles? You sell to MANY customers and "it is virtually impossible to collect every single dime." Reporting full A/R when you expect some to default would overstate assets → violates the principle of conservatism (don't overstate assets/revenue; recognize losses timely).
  • Two methods for uncollectibles: Direct Write-off and Allowance. Only the Allowance Method is GAAP-approved. (Direct write-off is still used by small/private/sole-proprietor businesses with very few bad debts.)
  • Direct write-off violates the Matching Principle: sale happens in Period 1, write-off (and the expense) lands in Period 2 — expense is disconnected from the revenue it helped generate.
  • Allowance Method fixes matching: records estimated Bad Debt Expense via an end-of-period adjusting entry, matching it to the same period's sales.
  • Allowance for Bad Debts (= Allowance for Doubtful Accounts — interchangeable terms; Wiley uses "Doubtful Accounts") is a contra-asset with a normal CREDIT balance. Why not just credit A/R directly? Because at estimate time you know the aggregate dollars likely uncollectible but not WHICH specific customer (subsidiary account) will default — so you use the Allowance as a holding place.
  • Net Realizable Value (NRV) = A/R − Allowance = the cash you realistically expect to collect. Shown on balance sheet as gross A/R, "Less: Allowance," = NRV.
  • Internal control: separate cash-handling from cash-accounting (credit) duties — otherwise a corrupt employee could write off a paying customer's account and pocket the cash.
  • Reducing collection risk: credit/debit cards (card company takes the risk for a fee), factoring (sell A/R outright to a third party for a fee — transfers risk), pledging (borrow money using A/R as collateral).
  • Note on LO 8.4 (Notes Receivable interest): the professor largely skips detailed exact-day interest computations. Required takeaway: a note bears interest, and interest accrues over time as the note stays outstanding.

Key Formulas & Rules

Allowance estimation — THREE methods (very testable):

Method Approach What you compute directly Base Unadjusted Allowance balance?
Percent-of-Sales Income Statement Bad Debt Expense directly Net credit sales IGNORED
Percent-of-Receivables Balance Sheet Target ending Allowance balance Ending A/R (single %) Used to find the plug
Aging-of-Receivables Balance Sheet Target ending Allowance balance Ending A/R by age bucket Used to find the plug
  • Percent-of-Sales: Bad Debt Expense = Net Credit Sales × Estimated %. Record that amount directly, regardless of any existing allowance balance. (Net credit sales = total credit sales − sales discounts − sales returns & allowances.)
  • Percent-of-Receivables / Aging (the "plug" logic):
  • Step 1: Target ending Allowance balance = Ending A/R × Estimated % (Aging: sum of each age bucket × its own %).
  • Step 2: Bad Debt Expense = the plug = amount needed to move the unadjusted Allowance balance to the target (credit) balance.
  • Unadjusted balance = beginning balance − write-offs during the period (can be a debit OR a credit).
  • The professor's #1 tip: DRAW THE T-ACCOUNT instead of memorizing. Put the target on the credit side, put the unadjusted balance where it sits, and the gap is your Bad Debt Expense. Works every time.
  • If unadjusted is a credit: plug = target − unadjusted credit (e.g., target $255, unadj. $55 cr → expense $200).
  • If unadjusted is a debit: plug = target + debit amount (e.g., target $185 cr, unadj. $55 dr → expense $240, because you must climb from −55 up to +185).
  • Aging logic: longer past-due = higher uncollectible %. (Textbook example: not-yet-due 1%, …, over-90-days as high as 90%.)
  • Notes Receivable interest: Interest = Principal × Rate × Time. Maturity Value = Principal + Interest.

Ratios:

  • Acid-Test (Quick) Ratio = (Cash + Short-Term Investments + Net Current Receivables) ÷ Total Current Liabilities — quick assets convert to cash in ~30 days; 1.0 or higher is generally safe. More stringent than current ratio (excludes inventory & prepaids).
  • A/R Turnover = Net Credit Sales ÷ Average Net A/R; Average Net A/R = (Beginning + Ending) ÷ 2. Higher = faster collection.
  • Days' Sales in Receivables (collection period) = 365 ÷ A/R Turnover. Shorter = better. (Turnover of 60 → ~6 days.)

Journal Entry Examples

Credit sale (always name the customer): Dr. Accounts Receivable—Customer / Cr. Sales Revenue

A/R converted to N/R (customer can't pay, signs a note): Dr. Notes Receivable—Company B / Cr. Accounts Receivable—Company B

Notes Receivable — interest accrues / collected at maturity:

  • Accrue: Dr. Interest Receivable / Cr. Interest Revenue
  • At maturity: Dr. Cash / Cr. Notes Receivable (principal) and Cr. Interest Receivable (interest)

DIRECT WRITE-OFF METHOD

  • Write-off (only when 100% sure): Dr. Bad Debt Expense 200 / Cr. Accounts Receivable—Customer #1 200
  • Recovery — Step 1 (reinstate): Dr. Accounts Receivable—Customer #1 200 / Cr. Bad Debt Expense 200
  • Recovery — Step 2 (collect): Dr. Cash 200 / Cr. Accounts Receivable—Customer #1 200
  • Income statement effect: reversing the expense raises net income by $200 (assets ↑ $200, equity ↑ $200).

ALLOWANCE METHOD (GAAP)

  • End-of-period adjusting entry (set up allowance): Dr. Bad Debt Expense 200 / Cr. Allowance for Bad Debts 200
  • Actual write-off (NO income statement effect — both are balance-sheet accounts): Dr. Allowance for Bad Debts 200 / Cr. Accounts Receivable—Customer #1 200
  • Recovery — Step 1 (reinstate): Dr. Accounts Receivable—Customer #1 200 / Cr. Allowance for Bad Debts 200
  • Recovery — Step 2 (collect): Dr. Cash 200 / Cr. Accounts Receivable—Customer #1 200
  • Recovery under allowance has NO net income statement effect (all balance-sheet accounts).

Worked allowance example (percent-of-sales): Net credit sales $60,000 × 0.5% = Dr. Bad Debt Expense 300 / Cr. Allowance for Bad Debts 300.

Worked plug example (percent-of-receivables): Ending A/R $6,375 × 4% = $255 target; unadjusted $55 credit → plug $200: Dr. Bad Debt Expense 200 / Cr. Allowance for Bad Debts 200.

Write-off exceeds allowance (rare): Need to write off $300 but allowance is only $200. Entry is the same — Dr. Allowance for Bad Debts 300 / Cr. Accounts Receivable 300 — leaving a temporary $100 DEBIT balance in the Allowance ("we underestimated; we owe ourselves a catch-up"). This is corrected at the next adjusting entry, NOT at write-off (e.g., to bring Allowance back to a $0 target: Dr. Bad Debt Expense 100 / Cr. Allowance for Bad Debts 100). Rule: a write-off NEVER touches Bad Debt Expense under the allowance method — expense only flows through the adjusting entry.

Common Mistakes

  • Debiting Bad Debt Expense at write-off under the allowance method. Wrong — write-off is Dr. Allowance / Cr. A/R. The expense was already recorded in the prior adjusting entry; write-off has no income statement effect.
  • Thinking a write-off changes NRV. It does NOT. You reduce A/R and Allowance by the same amount, so NRV is unchanged (e.g., $1,000 − $200 = $800 before; $800 − $0 = $800 after).
  • Treating percent-of-receivables / aging like percent-of-sales. With the receivables/aging methods you compute the target Allowance balance first, then plug the expense. You do NOT record the target amount as the expense.
  • Ignoring the unadjusted allowance balance in the plug methods — or using it in percent-of-sales (where it must be ignored).
  • Mishandling a DEBIT unadjusted balance. From a $55 debit to a $185 credit target the expense is $240, not $130. You must cover the negative AND reach the positive target.
  • Using total sales instead of NET CREDIT SALES for percent-of-sales and for A/R turnover.
  • Confusing A/R and N/R: only the note bears interest and has a formal written document.
  • Forgetting the customer name on credit-sale and write-off entries (subsidiary ledger detail).
  • Forgetting the two-step recovery: Step 1 reinstate the account, Step 2 collect the cash — don't skip straight to Dr. Cash.

Exam Checklist

  • ☐ State the differences between A/R and N/R (term length, written document, interest).
  • ☐ Convert A/R → N/R: Dr. N/R / Cr. A/R.
  • ☐ Explain why direct write-off violates the matching principle; why allowance is GAAP.
  • ☐ Record the allowance adjusting entry: Dr. Bad Debt Expense / Cr. Allowance.
  • ☐ Record an allowance-method write-off (Dr. Allowance / Cr. A/R) and explain no I/S effect + NRV unchanged.
  • ☐ Record a recovery (two steps) under both methods; know which one hits the income statement (direct) vs not (allowance).
  • ☐ Compute Bad Debt Expense by percent-of-sales (Net Credit Sales × %, ignore allowance balance).
  • ☐ Compute the target balance and plug expense for percent-of-receivables and aging — practice both credit and debit unadjusted balances; draw the T-account.
  • ☐ Build/read an aging schedule (sum buckets × their %s → target).
  • ☐ Know Allowance is a contra-asset, normal credit balance, and NRV = A/R − Allowance.
  • ☐ Handle the write-off > allowance case (same entry → temporary debit balance, fixed next adjusting entry).
  • ☐ Identify income-statement vs balance-sheet approach for each estimation method.
  • ☐ Compute the three ratios (acid-test, A/R turnover, days' sales) and interpret (1.0 safe; higher turnover / fewer days = better).
  • ☐ Know factoring vs pledging and the internal-control segregation-of-duties point.
  • ☐ N/R takeaway only: Interest = Principal × Rate × Time, Maturity Value = Principal + Interest (detailed day-count math is skipped).
