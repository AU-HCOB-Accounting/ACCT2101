Chapter 3 — The Adjusting Process

Exam-review summary. Built from the video lectures (Lectures 9–11); the textbook confirms structure and terminology only.

Key Concepts

  • Where adjusting entries fit in the cycle. Transaction analysis → journalize → post to ledger → unadjusted trial balance → adjusting entries → post → adjusted trial balance → financial statements. The professor stresses that the trial balance you learned in Chapter 2 is really the unadjusted one — it's prepared before any end-of-period adjustments.
  • Accrual vs. cash basis. Under accrual accounting, revenue is recognized when earned and expenses when incurred — cash timing does not dictate recognition. Under cash basis, revenue/expense follow the cash. GAAP requires accrual; cash basis is only for small private companies (sole proprietors, local retailers). This course = GAAP, so think accrual.
  • Why adjustments exist. Some economic events have no visible daily transaction or invoice — e.g., insurance expiring with time, revenue being earned over a subscription period. The professor repeats this: you must update the books at period-end to reflect what was really earned/incurred.
  • The "cash timing" memory hook (emphasized in all three lectures): Deferrals = cash FIRST, recognize later. Accruals = recognize FIRST, cash later. Four types: Deferred Revenue, Deferred Expense, Accrued Revenue, Accrued Expense.
  • Time period (periodicity) concept. Over an infinite horizon, cash-basis and accrual-basis totals are identical (the $600 example reaches $600 either way). The only reason timing matters is that companies must report on periodic (quarterly/annual) windows. This is why accrual accounting and adjusting entries are needed at all.
  • Revenue Recognition Principle (ASC 606, in effect since 2018). Five steps: (1) identify the contract, (2) identify the performance obligations, (3) determine the transaction price, (4) allocate the price to the obligations, (5) recognize revenue when/as each obligation is satisfied. Old rule was simply "recognize when earned."
  • Matching Principle (expense recognition). Match expenses to the period in which they helped generate revenue. The professor explicitly warns: matching is about timing, not matching equal dollar amounts of revenue and expense.
  • Depreciation concepts. Cost is recorded as an asset; as the asset is used, cost is allocated to expense via Depreciation Expense, credited to the contra-asset account Accumulated Depreciation (the Equipment account itself is never reduced). Book Value (Carrying Value) = Cost − Accumulated Depreciation. Original cost never changes; only Accumulated Depreciation grows.
  • Impact of omitting adjustments. Skipping an adjusting entry misstates both statements. Lecture 11 drills the deferred-expense case: omitting it leaves assets overstated, expenses understated, and net income / equity overstated.

Key Formulas & Rules

  • Three defining traits of EVERY adjusting entry (the professor's checklist — verify all three): 1. Recorded at the end of the period, right before financial statements. 2. Hits exactly one income statement account (a revenue or an expense) and exactly one balance sheet account (an asset or a liability). 3. NEVER involves Cash. If your adjusting entry touches Cash, it is wrong.
  • Supplies Expense = Beginning balance + Purchases − Ending physical count. (Books say $150, physical count $30 → $120 used.)
  • Straight-Line Depreciation = (Cost − Salvage/Residual Value) ÷ Useful Life. The numerator (Cost − Salvage) is the Depreciation Base — do not confuse it with Book Value.
  • Book Value = Cost − Accumulated Depreciation.
  • Interest = Principal × Rate × Time. The rate given is always annual — multiply by the fraction of the year (e.g., × 1/12 for one month).
  • Partial-year / partial-month depreciation: the formula gives the annual amount; divide by 12 for a monthly adjustment (e.g., $3,600/yr ÷ 12 = $300 for one month).
  • Step-4 revenue allocation: allocate the lump-sum price by each item's share of total standalone value (Service A $100 of $150 = 67% × $120 lump = $80, not $100).

Journal Entry Examples

Deferred Revenue — Unearned Service Revenue (magazine subscription, $1,200/yr collected Oct 1; 3 months earned by Dec 31 = $300)

  • Initial (Oct 1): Dr. Cash 1,200 / Cr. Unearned Service Revenue 1,200
  • Adjusting (Dec 31): Dr. Unearned Service Revenue 300 / Cr. Service Revenue 300
  • Unearned Service Revenue is a LIABILITY — settled by performing services, not by paying cash.

Deferred Expense — Prepaid Insurance ($1,200/yr bought Oct 1; 3 months expired = $300)

  • Initial (Oct 1): Dr. Prepaid Insurance 1,200 / Cr. Cash 1,200
  • Adjusting (Dec 31): Dr. Insurance Expense 300 / Cr. Prepaid Insurance 300 → ending Prepaid Insurance = $900 (9 months left)

Deferred Expense — Prepaid Rent (Smart Touch Learning, $3,000 for 3 months, Dec 1, 2026; 1 month used)

  • Initial (Dec 1): Dr. Prepaid Rent 3,000 / Cr. Cash 3,000
  • Adjusting (Dec 31): Dr. Rent Expense 1,000 / Cr. Prepaid Rent 1,000 → Prepaid Rent = $2,000

Deferred Expense — Supplies (Beg $50 + Purchases $100 = $150; physical count $30)

  • Adjusting (Dec 31): Dr. Supplies Expense 120 / Cr. Supplies 120

Deferred Expense — Depreciation (equipment $10,000, 10-yr life, $0 salvage = $1,000/yr)

  • Adjusting (Dec 31): Dr. Depreciation Expense 1,000 / Cr. Accumulated Depreciation 1,000
  • Partial-month (Smart Touch furniture $18,000, 5-yr, $0 salvage, acquired Dec 2 → $3,600/12 = $300): Dr. Depreciation Expense—Furniture 300 / Cr. Accumulated Depreciation—Furniture 300

Accrued Revenue (Smart Touch e-learning, $1,600/mo, service from Dec 16; half month = $800)

  • Adjusting (Dec 31): Dr. Accounts Receivable 800 / Cr. Service Revenue 800
  • Cash collection (Jan 15): Dr. Cash 1,600 / Cr. Accounts Receivable 800 / Cr. Service Revenue 800

Accrued Expense — Salaries (Smart Touch, $2,400/mo; Dec 16–31 earned = $1,200)

  • Adjusting (Dec 31): Dr. Salaries Expense 1,200 / Cr. Salaries Payable 1,200
  • Payment (Jan 1): Dr. Salaries Payable 1,200 / Cr. Cash 1,200

Accrued Expense — Interest ($60,000 building loan, 2% annual, 1 month)

  • Calc: $60,000 × 2% × 1/12 = $100
  • Adjusting (Dec 31): Dr. Interest Expense 100 / Cr. Interest Payable 100

Common Mistakes

  • Putting Cash in an adjusting entry. The single biggest red flag the professor names — an adjusting entry NEVER uses Cash. If Cash appears, it's a transaction entry, not an adjustment.
  • Forgetting the rate is annual. Lecture 11 calls this "one of the most common errors" — you must multiply by the fraction of the year (×1/12 for a month). Same trap with depreciation: divide the annual amount by 12 for partial-month adjustments.
  • Writing the same account name on both sides. Don't put "Insurance / Insurance" or "Supplies / Supplies." You must distinguish the expense (income statement) from the asset (balance sheet) — e.g., Insurance Expense vs. Prepaid Insurance, Supplies Expense vs. Supplies.
  • Crediting the Equipment account directly for depreciation. Wrong. Credit Accumulated Depreciation (contra-asset); the Equipment cost never changes.
  • Confusing Depreciation Base with Book Value. Depreciation Base = Cost − Salvage (the numerator). Book Value = Cost − Accumulated Depreciation. Different concepts.
  • Treating the initial cash transaction as an adjusting entry. The Dec 1 prepayment / the Oct 1 cash receipt is a standard transaction; the adjustment happens only at period-end (Dec 31).
  • Misjudging "what type of account." Unearned (Service) Revenue is a liability, not revenue, until earned. Accumulated Depreciation is a contra-asset with a credit normal balance.
  • Mixing up deferral vs. accrual direction. If terminology trips you up, the professor's advice: focus on the journal entry and whether the balance-sheet side is an asset or a liability, and remember "cash first (deferral)" vs. "recognize first (accrual)."
  • Wrong impact analysis when an entry is omitted. For an omitted deferred expense: assets overstated, expenses understated, net income and equity overstated — not the reverse.

Exam Checklist

You should be able to:

  • Differentiate cash-basis from accrual-basis accounting and explain why GAAP requires accrual.
  • Apply the time period concept, the revenue recognition principle (the 5 steps incl. price allocation), and the matching principle, and explain why timing matters.
  • Identify which of the four categories a situation is (deferred revenue, deferred expense, accrued revenue, accrued expense) using the cash-timing hook.
  • Journalize the adjusting entry for each type — prepaid insurance/rent, supplies (via physical count), depreciation, unearned revenue, accrued revenue, accrued salaries, and accrued interest — and verify it against the three rules (period-end, one I/S + one B/S account, no Cash).
  • Compute the amounts: months elapsed × per-month rate; Supplies Expense from beginning + purchases − ending count; straight-line depreciation (annual and partial-period); interest = principal × annual rate × fraction of year.
  • Distinguish the supplies treatment (credit the asset directly) from depreciation (credit Accumulated Depreciation, a contra-asset), and compute Book Value = Cost − Accumulated Depreciation.
  • Prepare/interpret an adjusted trial balance and trace an account through a worksheet (unadjusted balance → adjustment → adjusted balance), confirming debits = credits.
  • Analyze the impact of recording vs. omitting an adjusting entry on assets, liabilities, equity, expenses/revenues, and net income (over/understated).
  • (Lighter) Recognize the alternative treatment of deferrals (Appendix 3A) — record the full payment as expense / full receipt as revenue first, then reverse the unused/unearned portion; ending balances are identical. The professor says this is not heavily tested.

Chapter 4 — Completing the Accounting Cycle

Exam-prep review aid (~10–15 min). High-signal only. Closing entries are the final step that completes the accounting cycle — the professor stresses that Chapters 1–4 are the foundation for the entire course, so master this.

Key Concepts

Where Ch 4 sits in the cycle. The professor walks through the whole cycle: beginning balances → analyze transactions → journalize → post to ledger → unadjusted trial balance (Ch 1–2) → adjusting entries → adjusted trial balance (Ch 3) → prepare financial statements → closing entries → post-closing trial balance (Ch 4). Key emphasis: preparing financial statements is NOT the last step — you still have to close, then run a post-closing trial balance.

Financial statements are prepared in a fixed order (they're interrelated): Adjusted Trial Balance → Income Statement → Statement of Retained Earnings → Balance Sheet.

  • Net Income flows from the Income Statement into the Statement of Retained Earnings.
  • Ending Retained Earnings flows from that statement into the Balance Sheet.
  • That's why this order is required — you can't do the balance sheet until R/E is finalized.

Temporary vs. Permanent accounts (the central distinction of the chapter):

  • Temporary = Revenues, Expenses, Dividends, and Income Summary. These get closed to zero each period. Revenues/expenses are Income Statement items; Dividends lives on the Statement of Retained Earnings. They are closed because the income statement measures one specific period — if balances carried over, they'd show a multi-period accumulation instead of just this period's activity.
  • Permanent = Balance Sheet accounts (Assets, Liabilities, Common Stock, Retained Earnings). Balances carry forward: ending balance of one period = beginning balance of the next. Never closed.
  • Professor's framing: "closing" = resetting a balance to zero so the account can "start fresh" next period.

Income Summary account. A temporary "holding" account used only during closing. Has no normal balance and appears on no financial statement. Revenue and expenses are funneled through it; its balance after Steps 1–2 = Net Income (credit) or Net Loss (debit).

Retained Earnings is permanent — it is NOT closed. The closing process updates it (adds net income, subtracts dividends) but never resets it to zero. On the adjusted trial balance, R/E still shows the beginning balance; only after closing does it show the ending balance.

Classified Balance Sheet. Categorizes accounts. Assets listed in order of liquidity (how fast/easily converted to cash — most liquid = Cash first); liabilities listed by maturity (current vs. long-term). No current/long-term split exists for Stockholders' Equity.

  • Current assets: converted to cash, sold, or used up within 12 months OR the operating cycle, whichever is LONGER. (Cash, A/R, Inventory, Prepaids, Supplies.)
  • Operating cycle = "cash-to-cash" cycle: cash → buy goods/services → sell to customers → collect cash. Boeing example (professor's): airplanes take >1 year to build but are Boeing's inventory = current asset. The "operating cycle" definition is what lets long-to-produce inventory still count as current.
  • Long-term assets: everything not current. PP&E must be (1) long-lived, (2) tangible/physical substance, (3) used in operations. Land example: land held for speculation (buy low / sell high) is NOT PP&E — it's a long-term investment. Intent, not physical nature, decides. Intangibles = no physical form, valued for special rights (patents, copyrights).
  • Current liabilities: settled with cash OR goods/services within 1 year / operating cycle. (A/P, Salaries Payable, Interest Payable, Unearned Revenue — note: Unearned Revenue is settled by providing services, not cash.) Long-term liabilities: not due within a year (Bonds Payable, long-term Notes Payable, Mortgage Payable).

Worksheet (optional internal tool, not a financial statement, never given to outsiders). Extends the Ch 3 worksheet. When you pull revenues/expenses into the Income Statement columns, debits and credits do NOT equal — that's expected, because you only pulled part of the trial balance. The difference = Net Income. That same difference balances the Balance Sheet columns. (Professor's simplified worksheet lumps Dividends into the balance sheet section; a fuller worksheet adds a separate Statement of Retained Earnings column.)

Post-Closing Trial Balance = the official last step of the cycle. Prepared after closing entries are journalized and posted. Contains ONLY permanent accounts (assets, liabilities, equity). NO revenues, expenses, dividends, or Income Summary appear (all closed to zero). R/E shows the ending (updated) balance.

Current Ratio (performance evaluation): measures ability to pay current liabilities with current assets. Prefer high; benchmark > 1.0. Below 1.0 = current liabilities exceed current assets, a possible liquidity concern — but NOT automatic bankruptcy risk (strong operating cash flows can still cover obligations). Professor's contrast: unlike ROA (needs average total assets, because net income is earned over a period but assets are a point in time), the current ratio uses two balance-sheet figures both at the same point in time → no averaging needed.

Reversing Entries (Appendix — professor said this WILL NOT be tested). Optional entries on day 1 of the new period that exactly reverse certain accrual adjusting entries, purely for bookkeeping convenience. Same final result either way. Skip for the exam.

Key Formulas & Rules

  • Statement of Retained Earnings: Ending R/E = Beginning R/E + Net Income − Dividends (Net loss: subtract instead of add. A net loss decreases R/E.)

  • Net Income = Total Revenues − Total Expenses (= the Income Summary balance after Steps 1–2).

  • Current Ratio = Total Current Assets ÷ Total Current Liabilities (both from the balance sheet; no average).
  • Current asset/liability threshold: 12 months OR operating cycle, whichever is longer.
  • Closing direction rules (to zero out a normal balance, hit the opposite side):
  • Revenue: normal credit → debit it to close.
  • Expense: normal debit → credit it to close.
  • Dividends: normal debit → credit it to close.
  • Closing targets: Revenues & Expenses → Income Summary. Income Summary → Retained Earnings. Dividends → Retained Earnings DIRECTLY (never through Income Summary, because dividends are not part of the income statement / not expenses).

Journal Entry Examples

Professor's simple worked example: Revenue = $1,000, Expenses = $300, Dividends = $30, Beginning R/E = $50.

Step 1 — Close Revenue → Income Summary

Dr. Revenue              1,000
   Cr. Income Summary           1,000

Step 2 — Close Expenses → Income Summary (use one compound entry for all expense accounts rather than closing each separately)

Dr. Income Summary         300
   Cr. Expenses                   300

→ Income Summary now has a $700 credit balance = Net Income ($1,000 − $300).

Step 3 — Close Income Summary → Retained Earnings (net income case)

Dr. Income Summary         700
   Cr. Retained Earnings          700

If a net loss instead: Dr. Retained Earnings / Cr. Income Summary (loss decreases R/E).

Step 4 — Close Dividends → Retained Earnings (direct, NOT via Income Summary)

Dr. Retained Earnings       30
   Cr. Dividends                   30

Resulting Retained Earnings T-account: Beg $50 + N.I. $700 − Div $30 = Ending $720 — mirrors the Statement of Retained Earnings formula exactly.

(Textbook's larger parallel example — Smart Touch Learning: Service Revenue $17,500, total expenses $8,950 → Net Income $8,550; Dividends $5,000; Beg R/E $0 → Ending R/E $3,550. Same four-step pattern; e.g. Step 2 is one compound entry crediting all 8 expense accounts and debiting Income Summary $8,950.)

Common Mistakes

  • Thinking the financial statements are the last step. They aren't — you must still close, then prepare the post-closing trial balance. (Professor stresses this up front.)
  • Routing Dividends through Income Summary. Wrong — Dividends close directly to Retained Earnings. Putting them in Income Summary would distort net income (dividends aren't expenses).
  • Closing permanent accounts. Only temporary accounts (Rev, Exp, Dividends, Income Summary) are closed. Never close assets, liabilities, Common Stock, or Retained Earnings.
  • Treating Retained Earnings as temporary / resetting it to zero. It's permanent — closing updates it, never zeroes it.
  • Copying the adjusted-T/B Retained Earnings straight onto the balance sheet. That figure is the beginning balance. You must prepare the Statement of Retained Earnings first to get the ending balance.
  • Expecting the worksheet's Income Statement columns to balance. They won't — the debit/credit gap is the net income. Don't "fix" it.
  • Putting temporary accounts on the post-closing trial balance. It shows permanent accounts only.
  • Mixing up close directions. Revenue (credit normal) is debited to close; Expenses & Dividends (debit normal) are credited to close.
  • Using the wrong "whichever": current classification uses 12 months OR operating cycle, whichever is LONGER (not shorter).
  • Misclassifying speculative land as PP&E. If not used in operations, it's a long-term investment — intent governs.
  • Averaging the current ratio inputs. No averaging — both numbers are point-in-time balance-sheet figures (that's ROA, not this).
  • Reading a current ratio < 1.0 as automatic bankruptcy. It's a liquidity flag, not a death sentence.
  • Studying reversing entries for the exam. Professor explicitly said the reversing-entries appendix is not tested.

Exam Checklist

  • ☐ Recite the financial-statement order: Adj. T/B → I/S → Stmt of R/E → Balance Sheet, and what flows between them (Net Income; Ending R/E).
  • ☐ List temporary vs. permanent accounts from memory; know that Income Summary is temporary and R/E is permanent.
  • ☐ Perform all four closing steps with correct Dr./Cr. directions and correct targets (Dividends → R/E directly).
  • ☐ Know that Income Summary's post-Step-2 balance = Net Income (Cr.) or Net Loss (Dr.), and the Step 3 entry flips for a net loss.
  • ☐ Compute Ending R/E = Beg + N.I. − Dividends, and tie it to the closed R/E T-account.
  • ☐ State what does/doesn't appear on a post-closing trial balance (permanent only; R/E = ending balance).
  • ☐ Classify accounts on a classified balance sheet (current vs. long-term; assets by liquidity, liabilities by maturity; no split for equity).
  • ☐ Apply the "12 months OR operating cycle, whichever is longer" rule; explain the Boeing inventory example.
  • ☐ Distinguish PP&E from long-term investment by intent / use in operations (the land example).
  • ☐ Compute and interpret the Current Ratio (CA ÷ CL); know it's >1.0 preferred and uses no average (vs. ROA).
  • ☐ Recall the worksheet quirk: I/S columns don't balance; the gap = Net Income.
  • ☐ Order the full 9-step accounting cycle from beginning balances to post-closing trial balance.
  • ☐ Skip reversing entries — not on the exam.
