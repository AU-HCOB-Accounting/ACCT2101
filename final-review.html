Chapter 9 — Plant Assets, Natural Resources, and Intangibles

Scope note (from the professor): This chapter has many learning objectives, but for ACCT 2101 we focus on LO 1, 2, and 6 only. In practice that means: (1) measuring the cost of PP&E, (2) computing depreciation by all three methods (plus partial-year), and (6) the asset turnover ratio — capped off by a conceptual look at disposal. The professor explicitly skipped natural resources/depletion and intangibles/amortization/goodwill as too advanced for this course, so they are not testable from lecture. Study the items below.

Key Concepts

The three tests for PP&E (Property, Plant, and Equipment). An asset must meet all three to be PP&E:

  1. Long-term (non-current) — not converted to cash within one year / one operating cycle.
  2. Tangible — has physical substance (you can touch and see it).
  3. Used in operations — actively used in the business. A long-term tangible asset not used in operations is a long-term investment / other asset, NOT PP&E (e.g., land bought but never used for business).

Life cycle of a plant asset (the spine of the chapter): (1) Acquisition → (2) Usage (depreciation) → (3) Disposal.

Cost Principle / Capitalization. A plant asset is recorded at historical cost = purchase price plus all costs to get the asset ready for its intended use. Every such related cost is capitalized (debited to the asset, not expensed).

  • Land cost includes: purchase price, brokerage commissions, survey & legal fees, delinquent property taxes, title transfer fees, and cost of clearing/removing an old building. (Don't memorize the list — understand the "ready for intended use" concept.)
  • Buildings: if constructed → architectural fees, permits, contractor charges, materials/labor. If purchased → purchase price + renovation costs.
  • Machinery & Equipment: purchase price (less discounts), transportation/freight, insurance in transit, sales tax, purchase commission, installation, and testing costs. (Professor emphasized installation & testing — needed to get it running, so capitalized.)
  • Furniture & Fixtures: purchase price + costs to ready it (shipping, assembly).

Land vs. Land Improvements (key distinction).

  • Land = permanent, infinite life → NEVER depreciated.
  • Land Improvements (fences, paving, lighting, signs) = limited life → ARE depreciated, in a separate account.

Lump-sum (basket) purchase. One price for several assets together (usually a discount). Must split the price into separate accounts using the relative-market-value method: allocate by each asset's % of total appraised fair market value. Total debits = price actually paid, not total appraised value.

Capital vs. Revenue expenditures (during usage).

  • Capital expenditure (extraordinary repair) — increases capacity/efficiency or extends useful life → capitalize (debit the asset). Ex: $3,000 to rebuild a truck engine → debit Truck.
  • Revenue expenditure (ordinary repair) — routine maintenance, keeps asset in normal working order → expense now. Ex: $500 to replace tires → debit Repairs & Maintenance Expense.

Depreciation = allocation, not valuation. A systematic and rational process of allocating an asset's cost to expense over its useful life. Driven by the matching principle. It is NOT about fair market value and has nothing to do with cash. All plant assets except land are depreciated; assets can also become obsolete before physically wearing out.

Three depreciation factors: (1) Capitalized Cost — a known fact; (2) Estimated Useful Life — an estimate; (3) Estimated Residual (Salvage) Value — an estimate. Only cost is certain.

Two terms students confuse:

  • Depreciable Cost = Cost − Residual Value → the maximum total depreciation over the asset's whole life (does not change).
  • Book Value = Cost − Accumulated Depreciation → decreases every year. At end of life, accumulated dep. = depreciable cost, so book value = residual value.

Asset Turnover Ratio (LO 6). Net sales generated per average dollar of total assets — measures efficiency; higher is better. Uses average total assets because the balance sheet is a point-in-time snapshot while sales span the whole year.

Disposal (conceptual). Sell/discard an asset: (1) update depreciation to the disposal date; (2) remove the asset (credit asset at cost) and its Accumulated Depreciation (debit); (3) the cash rarely equals book value, so plug with a Gain or Loss.

Key Formulas & Rules

Straight-Line (equal each year):

Straight-Line Dep. = (Cost − Residual Value) ÷ Useful Life

Units-of-Production (varies with usage):

Step 1: Dep. per Unit = (Cost − Residual Value) ÷ Total Estimated Units
Step 2: Dep. Expense  = Dep. per Unit × Actual Units Used This Year

Double-Declining-Balance (accelerated — front-loaded):

DDB Dep. = Beginning Book Value × (2 ÷ Useful Life)
   where Beginning Book Value = Cost − Accumulated Depreciation
  • Residual value is NOT subtracted in the DDB formula (the one method that ignores it up front).
  • Final-year plug: if the formula would push book value below residual value, STOP and use Final-Year Dep. = Beginning Book Value − Residual Value to land exactly on residual value.

Partial-year depreciation (modified half-month convention — NOT formal GAAP, just a consistent convention):

Partial-Year Dep. = Annual Dep. × (Months in Service ÷ 12)
  • Purchased on/before the 15th → count that whole month. Purchased after the 15th → skip that month, start next month. Also applies to disposals (depreciate only to disposal date).

Revised estimate (textbook supplement):

Revised Dep. = (Remaining Book Value − New Residual Value) ÷ New Remaining Useful Life

Applied to current & future years only — prior years are never restated.

Asset Turnover Ratio:

Asset Turnover = Net Sales Revenue ÷ Average Total Assets
Average Total Assets = (Beginning Total Assets + Ending Total Assets) ÷ 2

Depletion / Amortization (terminology only — out of scope for this lecture): Natural resources (mines, oil) use depletion, computed like units-of-production: Depletion per Unit = (Cost − Residual) ÷ Total Estimated Units. Intangibles with limited life use amortization (usually straight-line). Goodwill and indefinite-life intangibles are not amortized but tested for impairment. The professor did not cover these computationally — do not expect lecture-based problems on them.

Dr. Land                       25,000

Dr. Truck                       3,000     (capital exp. — engine rebuild, extends life)
   Cr. Cash                          3,000

Dr. Repairs and Maintenance Exp.  500     (revenue exp. — tires, routine)
   Cr. Cash                            500

Dr. Depreciation Expense—Truck       8,000

Dr. Depreciation Expense—Truck       4,000
   Cr. Accumulated Depreciation—Truck     4,000

Dr. Cash                           20,000

   Cr. Truck                            41,000
   Cr. Gain on Disposal                  3,000   (cash $20k > BV $17k)

7) Disposal at a LOSS — same asset, sold for only $14,000:

Dr. Cash                           14,000
Dr. Accumulated Depreciation—Truck 24,000
Dr. Loss on Disposal                3,000        (cash $14k < BV $17k)
   Cr. Truck                            41,000

Common Mistakes

  • Expensing capitalizable costs. Brokerage, survey, installation, testing, clearing, freight, etc. are part of the asset's cost — capitalize them, don't expense.
  • Depreciating land. Land is never depreciated. Watch for Land Improvements hidden in a problem (fences/paving/lights) — those ARE depreciated.
  • Recording basket assets at appraised value. FMV is only used for the ratio. Total debits = price actually paid ($100,000), not total appraisal ($120,000).
  • Capital vs. revenue mix-up. Wrong classification overstates/understates both net income and assets. Ask: "Does it extend life or boost capacity?" Yes → capitalize; No → expense.
  • DDB: subtracting residual value in the formula. DDB does NOT subtract residual — multiply full book value × (2 ÷ life) in year 1.
  • DDB: missing the final-year plug. Mechanically applying the rate in the last year drops BV below residual. Use Beginning BV − Residual instead. (Professor: "the most common mistake students make.")
  • Book value computed off the prior year's book value. Always BV = original Cost − total Accumulated Depreciation, not last year's BV minus this year's expense.
  • Forgetting to update depreciation before disposal. Catch up depreciation to the disposal date first, or the gain/loss is wrong.
  • Gain/loss direction. Cash > BV = Gain (credit); Cash < BV = Loss (debit). Compare to book value, not to cost.
  • Asset turnover using ending (not average) assets, or forgetting to divide by 2.

Exam Checklist

  • ☐ State the 3 tests for PP&E; classify land "bought but unused" correctly (investment, not PP&E).
  • ☐ Build an asset's capitalized cost from a list of charges (know what's in vs. out).
  • ☐ Land vs. Land Improvements — which depreciates, which doesn't.
  • ☐ Do a lump-sum allocation by relative market value; debit each asset its share of the price paid.
  • ☐ Classify and journalize capital vs. revenue expenditures.
  • ☐ Compute Depreciable Cost vs. Book Value and explain the difference.
  • ☐ Compute and journalize Straight-Line depreciation and build the schedule (BV ends at residual).
  • ☐ Compute Units-of-Production: per-unit rate × actual units; schedule.
  • ☐ Compute DDB: rate = 2 ÷ life, no residual up front, and the final-year plug to residual.
  • ☐ Recognize all three methods give the same total depreciation ($40,000 here) — only timing differs.
  • ☐ Prorate partial-year depreciation (×months/12; half-month convention rule).
  • ☐ Record disposal at a gain AND at a loss (update dep. → remove cost & accum. dep. → plug gain/loss).
  • ☐ Compute the Asset Turnover Ratio = Net Sales ÷ Average Total Assets; interpret higher = better.
  • ☐ Remember Land is reported at full cost (no accumulated depreciation) on the balance sheet.

Chapter 11 — Current Liabilities

Exam-review summary. Note: the professor dropped the payroll-transactions learning objective (LO 2) as too complicated, so detailed payroll journal entries are not tested. Everything else in the chapter is fair game.

Key Concepts

What a liability is. A debt owed to creditors. Three characteristics: (1) arises from a past transaction/event, (2) creates a present obligation to pay cash or provide a service, (3) is unavoidable. Settled by either paying cash or providing a service.

Current vs. long-term. Current liabilities must be paid within one year OR the operating cycle, whichever is longer. Long-term (non-current) liabilities — long-term notes payable, mortgage payable, bonds payable — fall outside that window (Chapter 12).

Three categories of current liabilities (by certainty of amount):

  1. Known amount — accounts payable (from purchases), sales tax payable (from sales), unearned revenue (from customer advances), short-term notes payable (from issuing a note), income tax payable, current portion of long-term debt.
  2. Estimated amount — exists for sure, but exact dollar amount unknown: bonus plans, vacation pay, health/pension benefits, warranties.
  3. Contingent — depends on a future event: lawsuits, etc.

Three guiding questions the professor asks for every liability: (1) How do we settle it — cash or service? (2) How do we calculate interest (if any)? (3) How and when do we record it?

Sales tax vs. income tax — the key distinction. Sales tax is NOT an expense; the company is just a collection agent that remits it to the government. Income tax IS a true expense because it is levied on the company's own income.

Notes payable — maker vs. payee. The maker/issuer borrows money → records Notes Payable (this chapter's focus). The payee lends → records Notes Receivable. Notes Payable is always recorded and removed at face value; accrued interest sits in a separate Interest Payable account.

Unearned revenue is settled only by providing the promised service, never by paying cash back. Recognize revenue only as the performance obligation is satisfied.

Warranties & the matching principle. Record Warranty Expense in the same period as the related sales revenue (estimated from historical data at the point of sale) — not when claims come in later. Conceptually parallel to Accounts Receivable / Allowance for Bad Debts / Bad Debt Expense from Chapter 8.

Contingent liability = a potential liability depending on a future event. Treatment depends entirely on likelihood (remote / reasonably possible / probable).

Times-interest-earned (interest-coverage) ratio measures ability to pay interest expense. High = easily covers interest; below 1 = not enough operating income to cover interest (distress).

Key Formulas & Rules

Interest on a note:

Interest = Principal × Annual Rate × Time - The rate is almost always ANNUAL. Time must be a fraction of a year. - Term in days → days / 365 (e.g., 90/365). - Term in months → months / 12 (e.g., 9/12). - Quick checks: $2,000 × 10% × 9/12 = $150; $5,000 × 12% × 60/365 = $98.63.

Bonus (when bonus = % of net income AFTER the bonus):

Bonus = (Net Income Before Bonus × Bonus %) ÷ (1 + Bonus %) - Example: ($315,000 × 0.05) ÷ 1.05 = $15,000.

Warranty estimate: Warranty Expense = Warranty % × Sales. Example: 3% × $50,000 = $1,500.

Contingent-liability recognition rules:

Likelihood Accrue (record)? Disclose in notes?
Remote No No (do nothing)
Reasonably possible No Yes
Probable, amount NOT estimable No Yes
Probable AND estimable Yes Yes

Rule of thumb: accrue only when both probable and reasonably estimable.

Times-interest-earned ratio:

Times-Interest-Earned = (Net Income + Income Tax Expense + Interest Expense) ÷ Interest Expense - Numerator = EBIT (Earnings Before Interest and Taxes) = Operating Income. Add tax and interest back because we want earnings available before interest was paid (and tax is computed after interest). - Kohl's 2016: ($673 + $384 + $327) ÷ $327 = $1,384 ÷ $327 = 4.23. - Kohl's 2015: ($867 + $482 + $340) ÷ $340 = 4.97. Both well above 1 = strong ability to pay interest.

Common Mistakes

  • Treating sales tax as revenue or as an expense. It is neither — only the $10,000 is revenue; the $600 is a liability. Sales tax is never an expense.
  • Confusing sales tax with income tax. Income tax IS an expense; sales tax is not.
  • Using the stated rate as the period rate. A "10% note" is 10% per year, not 10% over 90 days. Always multiply by the time fraction.
  • Wrong denominator for time. Days → /365, months → /12. Mixing these up is a classic error.
  • Recording interest inside Notes Payable. Principal stays in Notes Payable (at face value); accrued interest goes to Interest Payable, separately.
  • Forgetting the year-end interest accrual on a note that spans two periods — the matching principle requires it, even though no cash is paid yet.
  • Double-counting warranty expense. Expense is recorded at the sale (estimate). When a claim is honored, only reduce the liability (Dr. Estimated Warranty Payable / Cr. Merchandise Inventory) — no second expense.
  • Settling unearned revenue with cash. It is settled by performing the service, recognizing revenue as the obligation is satisfied.
  • Bonus formula: dividing by the wrong base. When bonus is "% of income AFTER the bonus," divide by (1 + bonus %). Don't just take 5% × net income.
  • Contingent liabilities: accruing when only "reasonably possible," or accruing "probable" cases that can't be estimated. Accrue only when probable AND estimable.
  • TIE ratio: forgetting to add back income tax and interest to net income, or using net income alone in the numerator.

Exam Checklist

  • ☐ Define a liability (3 characteristics) and the current-vs-long-term cutoff (1 year or operating cycle, whichever is longer).
  • ☐ Classify a given liability as known / estimated / contingent.
  • ☐ Record sales tax (sale + remittance); know it's a liability, not an expense.
  • ☐ Record income tax payable; know it IS an expense (contrast with sales tax).
  • ☐ Record unearned revenue and recognize it as the service is performed.
  • ☐ Compute note interest with P × R × T; pick the right time fraction (/365 vs /12).
  • ☐ Journalize a note: issuance, year-end accrual (Interest Payable), and payment at maturity across two periods.
  • ☐ Distinguish maker (Notes Payable) vs. payee (Notes Receivable); record principal at face value.
  • ☐ Identify and reclassify the current portion of long-term debt.
  • ☐ Apply the bonus formula (÷ (1 + bonus %)) and record the bonus.
  • ☐ Accrue vacation/benefits (numbers given).
  • ☐ Warranty two-step: estimate/expense at sale, reduce liability at claim (no new expense).
  • ☐ Apply the contingent-liability table (remote / reasonably possible / probable / probable & estimable).
  • ☐ Compute and interpret the times-interest-earned ratio (EBIT ÷ interest expense); know above-1 = healthy.

Chapter 12 — Long-Term Liabilities

Professor's emphasis (read this first): This is a short chapter. The professor crossed out almost all of the bonds-payable learning objectives — bonds are only covered at a foundational level (issued at par). He explicitly said he is NOT doing premium/discount amortization in detail (that's an upper-level topic). The majority of the lecture, and the exam weight, is on long-term NOTES PAYABLE and MORTGAGES PAYABLE — building/reading amortization schedules and journalizing installment payments. Spend your study time there.

Key Concepts

  • Long-term liabilities = obligations NOT due within one year (or one operating cycle, whichever is longer). Reported in the long-term liability section of the balance sheet. Three types: (1) Bonds Payable, (2) Long-Term Notes Payable, (3) Mortgages Payable.

  • Bonds Payable (foundational only): A bond is like a big promissory note. It has a par value (face value) — the principal repaid at maturity — and a coupon rate (stated rate) — the % interest the issuer promises to pay each period. Company issues bond → receives cash → credits Bonds Payable. Pays periodic interest = par × coupon rate. At maturity: pays final interest plus repays full par value, clearing the liability.

  • Stated (coupon) vs. Market rate — determines issue price:

  • Coupon = Market → bond issued at PAR (cash received = face value). This is the only case the professor actually works.
  • Coupon < Market → bond issued at a DISCOUNT (cash received < face) — investors demand more, so they pay less.
  • Coupon > Market → bond issued at a PREMIUM (cash received > face) — bond pays better than market, so investors pay more.

  • (Professor mentions premium/discount exist but does NOT do the journal entries/amortization — don't over-study this.)

  • Long-Term Notes Payable: Borrow money, pay interest. Issuance entry is identical to a bond (Dr. Cash / Cr. Notes Payable). Repaid either as a lump sum at maturity or through serial installments that pay down principal over time. Interest must always be accrued over time regardless of repayment structure.

  • Amortization schedule: A table that splits each payment into interest (cost of borrowing) and principal (debt reduction), and tracks beginning and ending balance each period. Core skill for this chapter.

  • Interest is ALWAYS computed on the BEGINNING balance of the period: Beginning Balance × Rate × Time.

  • In the note example, the principal payment is FIXED ($5,000/yr), so the total cash payment DECREASES each year (because interest shrinks as the balance falls).

  • Mortgages Payable: A note payable secured by a specific asset (e.g., a building). Two key differences vs. a plain note: (1) secured by the property (lender can seize on default); (2) the TOTAL cash payment is FIXED each period.

  • Because the total payment is fixed while the balance falls: interest portion DECREASES and principal portion INCREASES over time. (Early payments are mostly interest; late payments are mostly principal.)

  • Record the asset and the financing at inception: Dr. Building / Cr. Cash (down payment) / Cr. Mortgage Payable (borrowed amount).

  • Debt to Equity Ratio: Measures financial leverage — how much of the assets are financed by debt vs. equity. Ratio > 1 = more debt than equity (higher leverage, higher risk).

Key Formulas & Rules

  • Bond interest payment (at par): Interest = Par Value × Coupon Rate

  • Example: $10,000,000 × 6% = $600,000 per period.

  • Issue price rule (stated vs. market):

  • Coupon = Market → Par (cash = face)
  • Coupon < Market → Discount (cash < face)

  • Coupon > Market → Premium (cash > face)

  • Amortization — interest expense (THE key formula): Interest Expense = Beginning Balance × Interest Rate × Time

  • Annual note, annual payment → Time = 1.

  • Monthly mortgage → use monthly rate = Annual Rate ÷ 12 (i.e., × 1/12).

  • Note (fixed principal): Total Payment = Fixed Principal + Interest on beginning balance. Ending Balance = Beginning Balance − Principal only (interest does NOT reduce the balance).

  • Mortgage (fixed total payment): Principal Reduction = Fixed Payment − Interest. Ending Balance = Beginning Balance − Principal Reduction.

  • Debt to Equity Ratio = Total Liabilities ÷ Total Equity (> 1 = debt-heavy / leveraged).

  • Excel (practical, "not required for exam"): =PMT(rate, nper, pv, fv) — rate = annual ÷ 12 for monthly, pv entered as negative. Goal Seek can also solve the payment by setting the final balance cell to 0.

Common Mistakes

  • Computing interest on the wrong balance. Interest is ALWAYS Beginning Balance × rate × time — NOT on the original loan amount every year, and NOT on the ending balance.
  • Subtracting the WHOLE payment from the balance. Only the principal portion reduces the balance. (Note: subtract $5,000, not the $6,200 total. Mortgage: subtract $99.60, not $599.98.)
  • Mixing up the date / beginning vs. ending balance. The ending balance on Dec 31 of one year = the beginning balance on Jan 1 of the next year (same number, same point in time).
  • Forgetting to convert to a monthly rate for mortgages. Use 6% ÷ 12 (× 1/12) per month — not the full annual 6%.
  • Confusing the two payment structures: note example = fixed principal (total payment falls); mortgage = fixed total payment (interest falls, principal rises).
  • Over-studying premium/discount bonds. The professor does NOT do the discount/premium amortization entries — don't waste exam-prep time there.
  • Debt-to-equity: it's Total Liabilities ÷ Total Equity (not ÷ total assets), and a ratio > 1 = debt-financed, not automatically "bad."

Exam Checklist

  • ☐ Bond AT PAR: issuance entry, periodic interest = par × coupon, maturity entry (interest + repay principal).
  • ☐ Know the stated vs. market rule: = par, < discount, > premium (concept only).
  • ☐ Build/read a note amortization schedule: interest = beg. bal × rate; fixed principal; total payment falls each year.
  • ☐ Journalize a note installment: Dr. Interest Expense, Dr. Notes Payable, Cr. Cash.
  • ☐ Mortgage: record building purchase (Dr. Building / Cr. Cash / Cr. Mortgage Payable); fixed payment; monthly rate = annual ÷ 12; interest falls & principal rises over time.
  • ☐ Journalize a mortgage payment: Dr. Interest Expense, Dr. Mortgage Payable, Cr. Cash.
  • ☐ Note vs. mortgage difference: mortgage is secured + fixed total payment.
  • ☐ Debt to Equity = Total Liabilities ÷ Total Equity; interpret > 1 vs. < 1 (leverage/risk).

Chapter 13 — Stockholders' Equity

Scope note (from the lecture): The professor states this course focuses exclusively on cash dividends. Stock dividends and stock splits are in the textbook but are not covered/tested here. Treasury stock is introduced as a category (issued but not outstanding) but no treasury-stock journal entry is worked in the transcript. Cumulative preferred / dividends-in-arrears is not worked in the lecture either. Those topics are included below at a terminology level only — confirm with your professor before relying on them for the exam.

Key Concepts

Two sources of stockholders' equity

  • Paid-in (Contributed) Capital — money actually invested by stockholders in exchange for stock (Common Stock + Paid-in Capital in Excess of Par/APIC). Externally generated.
  • Retained Earnings (Earned Capital) — accumulated net income not paid out. Internally generated.
  • Ending R/E = Beginning R/E + Net Income (− Net Loss) − Dividends.

Characteristics of a corporation (separate legal entity organized under state law)

  • Separate legal entity; no/essentially unlimited number of owners; no personal liability (limited to investment); lack of mutual agency (a single stockholder can't bind the corp); indefinite life; double taxation (corp pays tax on earnings, owners pay again on dividends); strong capital accumulation ability.
  • Main advantage: raise large capital + limited liability. Main disadvantage: double taxation.

Categories of stock (nested boxes)

  • Authorized = max shares the charter allows. Issued = actually sold (≤ authorized). Outstanding = held by outside investors. Treasury = repurchased by the company; it is issued but NOT outstanding.
  • Outstanding = Issued − Treasury.

Four stockholder rights: Vote (1 vote/share; elect Board of Directors), Dividends (only if/when declared by the board — never automatic), Liquidation (paid after creditors), Preemptive right (maintain proportional ownership; offered new shares first).

Classes of stock

  • Common = basic ownership, votes, dividends after preferred.
  • Preferred = priority on dividends (and liquidation); usually gives up voting rights. Carries a stated dividend rate (e.g., 6%).
  • Par value = arbitrary amount per share, no relation to market value (often $1 or $0.01). No-par = none assigned. Stated value = amount assigned to no-par stock; treated exactly like par for entries.

Issue price vs. market price: Issue price = what the corporation actually receives at first issuance. Daily market fluctuations afterward are NOT recorded on the balance sheet — the recorded amount doesn't change.

Cash dividends: Paid from retained earnings, never from paid-in capital. Legal capital = portion of equity (the paid-in/par amount) that legally can't be used for dividends. Paid only on outstanding shares (never on treasury).

EPS — the most widely used profitability metric; reported at the bottom of the income statement; measures net income earned for each common share.

Key Formulas & Rules

  • Outstanding = Issued − Treasury
  • Golden rule: Common (and Preferred) Stock is ALWAYS credited at PAR (or stated) value. Anything above par goes to Paid-in Capital in Excess of Par.
  • Premium per share = Issue price − Par. Total to APIC = Premium × shares.
  • No-par stock: entire cash received → Common Stock (no APIC).
  • Stock for non-cash assets: record asset at market value of stock issued OR asset received, whichever is more clearly determinable; Common Stock at par; difference to APIC.
  • Preferred annual dividend per share = Par × Stated rate. (e.g., $50 par × 6% = $3/share.)
  • Total preferred dividend = Par × rate × # preferred shares.
  • Cumulative preferred (terminology only): unpaid dividends accumulate as dividends in arrears (not a recorded liability until declared) and must be paid — all arrears + current year — before common gets anything. Not worked in this lecture.
  • EPS = (Net Income − Preferred Dividends) ÷ Average Common Shares Outstanding. Subtract preferred dividends to isolate earnings available to common; use average shares because net income is a flow over the whole period while share counts change.

Common Mistakes

  • Crediting Common Stock at the issue price instead of par. Common/Preferred Stock is ALWAYS at par; the excess goes to APIC.
  • Thinking the premium (APIC) is a gain/revenue. It is paid-in capital, not income — it never touches the income statement or retained earnings.
  • Recording a journal entry on the date of record. There is none — only declaration and payment.
  • Confusing issue price with market price — later market swings are never booked.
  • Forgetting dividends are paid only on outstanding shares (Issued − Treasury), not on issued or authorized shares.
  • Treating dividends as automatic — they require board declaration.
  • Paying dividends from paid-in capital — dividends come from retained earnings.
  • In EPS: forgetting to subtract preferred dividends, or using ending instead of average common shares.
  • Mixing up the equity effects: at declaration equity ↓ / liabilities ↑ / assets unchanged; at payment assets ↓ / liabilities ↓ / equity unchanged.
  • Balance-sheet order: Preferred is listed before Common (priority rights).
