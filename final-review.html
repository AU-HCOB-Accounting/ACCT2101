Chapter 9 — Plant Assets, Natural Resources, and Intangibles

📖 For worked examples and full detail, see the Study Guide — Chapter 9.

Scope note (from the professor): For ACCT 2101 we focus on LO 1, 2, and 6 only: (1) the cost of PP&E, (2) depreciation by all three methods (plus partial-year) and disposal, and (6) the asset turnover ratio. The professor skipped natural resources/depletion and intangibles/amortization/goodwill — they are not testable from lecture.

Key Ideas

  • Three tests for PP&E (must meet all): long-term (non-current), tangible (physical substance), and used in operations. A long-term tangible asset not used in operations is an investment, not PP&E.
  • Capitalize all costs to get the asset ready for its intended use (cost principle). Examples: land — commissions, legal/survey fees, delinquent taxes, clearing an old building; equipment — freight, insurance in transit, installation, testing. Understand the concept, don't memorize lists.
  • Land = infinite life → NEVER depreciated. Land Improvements (fences, paving, lights) = limited life → ARE depreciated, in a separate account.
  • Lump-sum (basket) purchase: split one price across assets by the relative-market-value method (each asset's % of total appraised FMV). Total debits = price actually paid, not total appraised value.
  • Capital vs. revenue expenditures: capital (extends useful life / boosts capacity) → capitalize; revenue (routine repair/maintenance) → expense now.
  • Depreciation = allocation, not valuation. A systematic process driven by the matching principle; nothing to do with fair market value or cash. All plant assets except land are depreciated.
  • Depreciable Cost = Cost − Residual (max total depreciation, fixed) vs. Book Value = Cost − Accumulated Depreciation (falls each year, ending at residual value).
  • Disposal: (1) update depreciation to the disposal date, (2) remove the asset's cost and its accumulated depreciation, (3) plug the difference with a Gain or Loss.

Key Formulas & Rules

  • Straight-line = (Cost − Residual) ÷ Useful life
  • Units-of-production = (Cost − Residual) ÷ Total units, then × units used this year
  • Double-declining-balance = Beginning Book Value × (2 ÷ Useful life). Residual is NOT subtracted; in the final year, plug down to residual (Final-year dep. = Beginning Book Value − Residual).
  • Partial-year dep. = Annual dep. × (Months in service ÷ 12). Purchased on/before the 15th = count the whole month; after the 15th = start next month.
  • Book Value = Cost − Accumulated Depreciation
  • Asset Turnover = Net Sales ÷ Average Total Assets, where Average Total Assets = (Beginning + Ending) ÷ 2

All three depreciation methods produce the same total depreciation over the asset's life — only the timing differs.

Must-Know Journal Entries

Depreciation (each period): Dr. Depreciation Expense / Cr. Accumulated Depreciation.

Disposal (pattern — plug to balance): Dr. Cash (amount received), Dr. Accumulated Depreciation (full balance), Cr. Asset (at cost), then Dr. Loss (if cash < book value) or Cr. Gain (if cash > book value) to balance.

Common Mistakes

  • Expensing capitalizable costs — freight, installation, testing, clearing, survey fees, etc. are part of the asset's cost; capitalize them.
  • Depreciating land — land is never depreciated. Watch for Land Improvements hidden in a problem; those ARE depreciated.
  • Recording basket assets at appraised value — FMV is only used for the allocation ratio. Total debits = price actually paid.
  • Capital vs. revenue mix-up — wrong classification misstates both net income and assets. Ask: "Does it extend life or boost capacity?" Yes → capitalize; No → expense.
  • DDB: subtracting residual in the formula — DDB ignores residual up front; multiply full book value × (2 ÷ life) in year 1.
  • DDB: missing the final-year plug — applying the rate mechanically in the last year drops book value below residual. Use Beginning BV − Residual instead. (Professor: the most common mistake.)
  • Computing book value off last year's BV — always BV = original Cost − total Accumulated Depreciation.
  • Forgetting to update depreciation before disposal — catch up to the disposal date first, or the gain/loss is wrong.
  • Gain/loss direction — compare cash to book value (not cost): cash > BV = Gain (credit); cash < BV = Loss (debit).
  • Asset turnover using ending (not average) assets, or forgetting to divide by 2.

Exam Checklist

  • ☐ You should be able to state the 3 tests for PP&E and classify land "bought but unused" as an investment, not PP&E.
  • ☐ You should be able to build an asset's capitalized cost from a list of charges (what's in vs. out).
  • ☐ You should be able to distinguish Land vs. Land Improvements — which depreciates, which doesn't.
  • ☐ You should be able to do a lump-sum allocation by relative market value and debit each asset its share of the price paid.
  • ☐ You should be able to classify and journalize capital vs. revenue expenditures.
  • ☐ You should be able to compute and explain Depreciable Cost vs. Book Value.
  • ☐ You should be able to compute Straight-Line depreciation and build a schedule that ends at residual.
  • ☐ You should be able to compute Units-of-Production: per-unit rate × actual units.
  • ☐ You should be able to compute DDB: rate = 2 ÷ life, no residual up front, with the final-year plug to residual.
  • ☐ You should be able to explain that all three methods give the same total depreciation — only timing differs.
  • ☐ You should be able to prorate partial-year depreciation (× months/12; half-month rule).
  • ☐ You should be able to record disposal at a gain AND at a loss (update dep. → remove cost & accum. dep. → plug).
  • ☐ You should be able to compute and interpret the Asset Turnover Ratio (higher = better).
  • ☐ You should remember land is reported at full cost with no accumulated depreciation.

Chapter 11 — Current Liabilities

📖 For worked examples and full detail, see the Study Guide — Chapter 11.

Exam-review summary. Note: the professor dropped the payroll-transactions learning objective (LO 2) as too complicated, so detailed payroll journal entries are not tested. Everything else in the chapter is fair game.

Key Ideas

  • Liability = a present obligation from a past event, settled by paying cash or providing a service.
  • Current vs. long-term: current liabilities are due within one year or the operating cycle, whichever is longer; everything else is long-term (Ch. 12).
  • Three categories: known amount (accounts payable, sales tax, unearned revenue, notes payable), estimated (warranties, bonuses, vacation pay), and contingent (depends on a future event, e.g. lawsuits).
  • Sales tax is a liability, NOT an expense — the company is just a collection agent remitting to the government. Income tax IS a true expense because it's levied on the company's own income.
  • Notes payable: the maker/issuer borrows and records Notes Payable (this chapter); the payee records Notes Receivable. Principal stays in Notes Payable at face value; accrued interest goes in a separate Interest Payable account.
  • Unearned revenue is settled by performing the service, never by refunding cash; recognize revenue as the obligation is satisfied.
  • Warranties follow the matching principle: record Warranty Expense (an estimate) in the same period as the sale, not when claims arrive.
  • Contingent liabilities are recognized based on likelihood (remote / reasonably possible / probable).

Key Formulas & Rules

  • Interest = Principal × Annual Rate × Time — the rate is almost always ANNUAL, so time is a fraction of a year: days ÷ 365, or months ÷ 12.
  • Bonus (on income after the bonus) = (Net Income Before Bonus × Bonus %) ÷ (1 + Bonus %)
  • Warranty Expense = Warranty % × Sales
  • Times-Interest-Earned = (Net Income + Income Tax Expense + Interest Expense) ÷ Interest Expense — numerator is EBIT (operating income); above 1 = healthy ability to cover interest.

Contingent-liability recognition:

Likelihood Accrue (record)? Disclose in notes?
Remote No No (do nothing)
Reasonably possible No Yes
Probable, amount NOT estimable No Yes
Probable AND estimable Yes Yes

Rule of thumb: accrue only when both probable and reasonably estimable.

Must-Know Journal Entries

  • Sales tax (at sale): Dr. Cash / Cr. Sales Revenue / Cr. Sales Tax Payable. Remit later: Dr. Sales Tax Payable / Cr. Cash.
  • Note payable — issuance: Dr. Cash (or Inventory) / Cr. Notes Payable.
  • Note payable — year-end interest accrual: Dr. Interest Expense / Cr. Interest Payable.
  • Note payable — payment at maturity: Dr. Notes Payable / Dr. Interest Payable (prior accrual) / Dr. Interest Expense (current) / Cr. Cash.
  • Unearned revenue: receive cash Dr. Cash / Cr. Unearned Revenue; earn it Dr. Unearned Revenue / Cr. Service Revenue.
  • Warranty — estimate at sale: Dr. Warranty Expense / Cr. Estimated Warranty Payable.
  • Warranty — honoring a claim (no new expense): Dr. Estimated Warranty Payable / Cr. Merchandise Inventory (or Cash).

(Payroll JEs are NOT tested — LO 2 was dropped by the professor.)

Common Mistakes

  • Treating sales tax as revenue or as an expense. It is neither — only the sale price is revenue; the tax collected is a liability.
  • Confusing sales tax with income tax. Income tax IS an expense; sales tax is not.
  • Using the stated rate as the period rate. A "10% note" is 10% per year, not over 90 days — always multiply by the time fraction.
  • Wrong denominator for time. Days → ÷365, months → ÷12. Mixing these up is a classic error.
  • Recording interest inside Notes Payable. Principal stays in Notes Payable at face value; accrued interest goes to Interest Payable, separately.
  • Forgetting the year-end interest accrual on a note spanning two periods — the matching principle requires it even though no cash moves yet.
  • Double-counting warranty expense. Expense is recorded at the sale. When a claim is honored, only reduce the liability — no second expense.
  • Settling unearned revenue with cash. It is settled by performing the service.
  • Bonus formula: when the bonus is "% of income AFTER the bonus," divide by (1 + bonus %) — don't just take the % of net income.
  • Contingent liabilities: accruing when only "reasonably possible," or accruing "probable" cases that can't be estimated. Accrue only when probable AND estimable.
  • TIE ratio: forgetting to add back income tax and interest to net income.

Exam Checklist

  • ☐ You should be able to define a liability and apply the current-vs-long-term cutoff (1 year or operating cycle, whichever is longer).
  • ☐ You should be able to classify a liability as known / estimated / contingent.
  • ☐ You should be able to record sales tax (sale + remittance) and know it's a liability, not an expense.
  • ☐ You should be able to record income tax payable and explain why it IS an expense (contrast with sales tax).
  • ☐ You should be able to record unearned revenue and recognize it as the service is performed.
  • ☐ You should be able to compute note interest with P × R × T, choosing the right time fraction (÷365 vs ÷12).
  • ☐ You should be able to journalize a note across two periods: issuance, year-end accrual (Interest Payable), and payment at maturity.
  • ☐ You should be able to distinguish maker (Notes Payable) from payee (Notes Receivable) and record principal at face value.
  • ☐ You should be able to identify and reclassify the current portion of long-term debt.
  • ☐ You should be able to apply the bonus formula (÷ (1 + bonus %)) and record the bonus.
  • ☐ You should be able to accrue vacation/benefits when numbers are given.
  • ☐ You should be able to do the warranty two-step: estimate/expense at sale, reduce liability at claim (no new expense).
  • ☐ You should be able to apply the contingent-liability table (remote / reasonably possible / probable / probable & estimable).
  • ☐ You should be able to compute and interpret the times-interest-earned ratio (EBIT ÷ interest expense); above 1 = healthy.

Chapter 12 — Long-Term Liabilities

📖 For worked examples and full detail, see the Study Guide — Chapter 12.

Scope note: The professor covers bonds only at PAR and does NOT do premium/discount amortization (upper-level topic). Exam weight is on long-term NOTES PAYABLE and MORTGAGES — building and reading amortization schedules and journalizing installment payments. Study time goes there.

Key Ideas

  • Long-term liabilities = obligations not due within one year. Three types: bonds payable, long-term notes payable, mortgages payable.
  • A bond is a large promissory note with a par (face) value repaid at maturity and a coupon (stated) rate for periodic interest.
  • Stated vs. market rate sets the issue price (concept only): coupon = market → par; coupon < market → discount; coupon > market → premium.
  • An amortization schedule splits each payment into interest (cost of borrowing, computed on the BEGINNING balance) and principal (debt reduction), tracking beginning and ending balances.
  • Note example = fixed principal: equal principal each period, so the total payment falls as interest shrinks.
  • Mortgage = secured + fixed total payment: backed by a specific asset; total payment is constant, so interest falls and principal rises over time (early payments mostly interest).
  • Debt-to-equity measures financial leverage — debt vs. equity financing; > 1 = debt-heavy / higher risk.

Key Formulas & Rules

  • Bond interest (at par) = Par × Coupon rate.
  • Issue price rule: coupon = market → par; coupon < market → discount; coupon > market → premium.
  • Interest Expense = Beginning Balance × Rate × Time (monthly mortgage: Annual Rate ÷ 12).
  • Note (fixed principal): Total Payment = Fixed Principal + Interest on beginning balance.
  • Mortgage (fixed total payment): Principal portion = Fixed Payment − Interest.
  • Ending Balance = Beginning Balance − Principal portion only (interest does NOT reduce the balance).
  • Debt to Equity = Total Liabilities ÷ Total Equity (> 1 = leveraged).

Must-Know Journal Entries

  • Bond issued at par: Dr. Cash / Cr. Bonds Payable.
  • Bond interest payment: Dr. Interest Expense / Cr. Cash.
  • Bond at maturity: Dr. Interest Expense, Dr. Bonds Payable / Cr. Cash.
  • Note or mortgage installment payment (identical structure): Dr. Interest Expense, Dr. Note/Mortgage Payable / Cr. Cash.
  • Mortgage at purchase: Dr. Building / Cr. Cash (down payment) / Cr. Mortgage Payable.

Common Mistakes

  • Computing interest on the wrong balance. Always Beginning Balance × rate × time — not on the original loan amount each year, and not on the ending balance.
  • Subtracting the whole payment from the balance. Only the principal portion reduces the balance, never the total payment.
  • Mixing up beginning vs. ending balance. One year's ending balance = next year's beginning balance.
  • Forgetting the monthly rate on mortgages. Use Annual Rate ÷ 12, not the full annual rate.
  • Confusing the two structures: note = fixed principal (total payment falls); mortgage = fixed total payment (interest falls, principal rises).
  • Over-studying premium/discount bonds. The professor skips those amortization entries.
  • Debt-to-equity errors: it's ÷ total Equity (not total assets), and > 1 means debt-financed, not automatically "bad."

Exam Checklist

  • ☐ You should be able to journalize a bond at par: issuance, periodic interest (par × coupon), and maturity (interest + repay principal).
  • ☐ You should be able to apply the stated vs. market rule: = par, < discount, > premium (concept only).
  • ☐ You should be able to build and read a note amortization schedule: interest on beginning balance, fixed principal, total payment falling each year.
  • ☐ You should be able to build and read a mortgage amortization schedule: fixed total payment, monthly rate = annual ÷ 12, interest falling and principal rising.
  • ☐ You should be able to journalize a note installment (Dr. Interest Expense, Dr. Notes Payable, Cr. Cash).
  • ☐ You should be able to journalize a mortgage purchase (Dr. Building / Cr. Cash / Cr. Mortgage Payable) and payment (Dr. Interest Expense, Dr. Mortgage Payable, Cr. Cash).
  • ☐ You should be able to explain the note vs. mortgage difference: mortgage is secured and has a fixed total payment.
  • ☐ You should be able to compute and interpret Debt to Equity = Total Liabilities ÷ Total Equity (> 1 vs. < 1, leverage/risk).

Chapter 13 — Stockholders' Equity

📖 For worked examples and full detail, see the Study Guide — Chapter 13.

Scope note (from the lecture): This course focuses exclusively on cash dividends. Stock dividends and stock splits are not covered/tested. Treasury stock and cumulative preferred / dividends-in-arrears appear at a terminology level only in the lecture — confirm with your professor before relying on them.

Key Ideas

  • Two sources of equity: Paid-in Capital (invested by stockholders: Common Stock + Paid-in Capital in Excess of Par) and Retained Earnings (accumulated net income not paid out).
  • Corporation traits: separate legal entity, limited liability, indefinite life, lack of mutual agency, and double taxation (corp taxed on earnings, owners taxed again on dividends). Main advantage = raise large capital + limited liability; main drawback = double taxation.
  • Share categories: Authorized (charter max) ≥ Issued (sold) ≥ Outstanding (held by investors). Treasury = repurchased shares, issued but not outstanding. Outstanding = Issued − Treasury.
  • Common vs. Preferred: Common votes and gets dividends after preferred. Preferred has dividend/liquidation priority, a stated rate, usually no vote. Preferred is listed before common on the balance sheet.
  • Par / no-par / stated value: Par is an arbitrary per-share amount unrelated to market value; stated value is treated exactly like par; no-par has none assigned.
  • Golden rule: Stock is always credited at par (or stated value); any excess goes to Paid-in Capital in Excess of Par. Later market price swings are never recorded.
  • Cash dividends are paid from retained earnings only, only on outstanding shares, and only when declared by the board. Three dates: declaration (entry), record (no entry), payment (entry).
  • EPS is the most-watched profitability metric, reported at the bottom of the income statement.

Key Formulas & Rules

  • Outstanding shares = Issued − Treasury
  • Stock credited at par; excess (Issue price − Par) × shares → Paid-in Capital in Excess of Par
  • No-par stock: entire cash received → Common Stock (no excess account)
  • Stock for non-cash asset: record at market value of stock or asset, whichever is more clearly determinable
  • Preferred dividend = Par × Stated rate × # preferred shares
  • EPS = (Net Income − Preferred Dividends) ÷ Average Common Shares Outstanding

Must-Know Journal Entries

Issue stock above par

Dr. Cash                                  (shares × issue price)
   Cr. Common Stock                              (shares × par)
   Cr. Paid-in Capital in Excess of Par          (excess)

Cash dividend — declaration

Dr. Cash Dividends                        xxx
   Cr. Dividends Payable                        xxx

Date of record — NO ENTRY.

Cash dividend — payment

Dr. Dividends Payable                     xxx
   Cr. Cash                                     xxx

Common Mistakes

  • Crediting Common Stock at the issue price instead of par — it's always at par, excess to Paid-in Capital in Excess of Par.
  • Thinking the excess over par is a gain/revenue — it's paid-in capital, never income.
  • Recording an entry on the date of record — there is none.
  • Forgetting dividends are paid only on outstanding shares (not issued or authorized).
  • Treating dividends as automatic — they require board declaration.
  • Paying dividends from paid-in capital — they come from retained earnings.
  • In EPS: forgetting to subtract preferred dividends, or using ending instead of average common shares.
  • Mixing up equity effects: at declaration equity ↓ / liabilities ↑; at payment assets ↓ / liabilities ↓.
  • Balance-sheet order: preferred before common.

Exam Checklist

  • ☐ You should be able to list corporation characteristics and explain double taxation and limited liability.
  • ☐ You should be able to distinguish authorized / issued / outstanding / treasury and compute Outstanding = Issued − Treasury.
  • ☐ You should be able to journalize issuing stock above par — stock at par, excess to Paid-in Capital in Excess of Par.
  • ☐ You should be able to identify the two sources of equity (paid-in capital vs. retained earnings) and order the equity section (preferred before common).
  • ☐ You should be able to compute a preferred dividend = Par × Stated rate × # preferred shares.
  • ☐ You should be able to record a cash dividend at declaration and payment, with no entry on the date of record, and know each step's accounting-equation effect.
  • ☐ You should be able to compute EPS = (Net Income − Preferred Dividends) ÷ Average Common Shares, and explain why (isolate common's share; average for fluctuating share counts).
  • ☐ You should remember the scope: cash dividends only; stock dividends/splits not tested.
